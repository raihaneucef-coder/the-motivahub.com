01 · Welcome

How to use this kit

This is not a book. Books sit on shelves. This is a worksheet you fill in with a pen, on the desk, in one sitting. Twenty to forty minutes, and you walk out with three habits you can run for 30 days.

The framework is small on purpose:

  1. Anchor — a habit you already do without thinking (coffee, shower, sitting down at desk).
  2. Stack — a new, tiny behavior attached to that anchor. Two minutes maximum.
  3. Friction — one thing in your environment that made the old attempt fail. Remove it.
Rules of the kit. Fill it in one pass — don't come back tomorrow. Under two minutes per new habit. Never miss twice in a row. Track on paper, not on an app for the first 30 days (apps are the reason your last 5 attempts failed).

Everything in this kit comes from applied behavior design (Clear, Fogg, Lally), 8 years of personal protocols, and the failure reports of people who tried and quit. Nothing is theory you'll never use.

02 · The Formula

The Habit Stack Formula

The full stack is one sentence. Memorize it:

After I [ANCHOR],
I will [TINY NEW HABIT].
So that I become [IDENTITY].

Example: "After I pour my morning coffee, I will write one sentence in my journal. So that I become someone who writes daily."

Notice three things:

  1. The anchor is unambiguous. Not "in the morning" — that's a mood. "After I pour my coffee" — that's a physical event you can point to.
  2. The new habit fits in two minutes. Not "write 500 words". One sentence. You can scale up later, but the entry bar must survive your worst day.
  3. The identity clause is stated out loud. Habits fail when they are only tasks. Reading "so that I become a writer" once makes the sentence feel like evidence for an argument, not a chore.
The full source of this method: James Clear, Atomic Habits — amazon.fr/dp/0735211290
03 · Anchor Audit

Find your anchors

Every day has 8-12 automatic behaviors you already do daily without willpower. These are your anchors. List them:

Morning (before noon)
Midday (noon to 5pm)
Evening (after 6pm)

Selection rule: pick 3 anchors max — one morning, one midday, one evening. If you pick more, you're building a schedule, not a stack. Schedules fail.

04 · Starter Stacks

Ten stacks that survive real weeks

Morning · After I pour my coffee, I write one sentence. → Becomes: someone who captures thinking before it evaporates.
Morning · After I brush my teeth, I do 10 squats before touching my phone. → Becomes: someone whose body moves first.
Morning · After I sit at my desk, I open ONE file, not my inbox. → Becomes: someone who starts with the work.
Midday · After I finish lunch, I walk 6 minutes outside — no phone. → Becomes: someone who resets the nervous system, not just eats.
Midday · After I close a call, I write one sentence about what was decided. → Becomes: someone whose meetings produce artifacts.
Evening · After I put my phone on the charger, I read 2 pages of a book. → Becomes: someone who ends the day with input, not output.
Evening · After I change into home clothes, I set tomorrow's top-3 on paper. → Becomes: someone who wakes with a plan.
Evening · After I brush my teeth at night, I write one thing that worked today. → Becomes: someone who sees evidence for the story "I'm getting better".
Transition · After I close my laptop, I do one 60-second stretch before standing. → Becomes: someone whose body doesn't pay for the desk hours.
Weekly · After Sunday coffee, I look at 7 days of my calendar for 5 minutes. → Becomes: someone who audits the week, not just survives it.
For the identity-based approach: James Clear, Atomic Habits — amazon.fr/dp/0735211290. For the tiny-behavior science: B.J. Fogg, Tiny Habits — amazon.fr/dp/059323484X.

06 · Friction Removal

Why your last 5 attempts failed

It was not motivation. It was that the phone was 30 cm closer than the journal. Behavior is mostly environment. Audit:

Bedroom

  • Phone charges outside arm's reach (or in another room)
  • Book is on the nightstand, not on a shelf
  • Clothes for tomorrow's workout are laid out visible
  • Alarm clock is a physical clock, not your phone

Desk

  • Only the current project is open on screen
  • Notifications disabled for non-human sources
  • Notebook is on top of keyboard, not in a drawer
  • Water bottle sits next to laptop (visual reminder)

Kitchen

  • Fruit is at eye level; snacks are in a closed container in a cupboard
  • Prep containers are visible on counter, not buried in a cabinet
  • Supplements sit next to the coffee maker (anchor proximity)
A physical alarm clock, cheap and effective: Philips Somneo — amazon.fr/s?k=somneo. Phone-locking box for deep-work hours: k-lock — amazon.fr/s?k=phone+locking+box.
07 · Environment Design

Habit stacking × environment

A stack without an environment is a wish. A stack inside a designed space is a machine. Combine them:

AnchorStackEnvironment tweak
Pour coffeeWrite 1 sentenceNotebook already open on counter
Brush teeth10 squatsMat unrolled in front of sink
Sit at deskOpen 1 fileBookmark on that file; inbox icon removed
Close laptop60-second stretchYoga block sits on the laptop itself
Change clothesWrite top-3Pen taped to wardrobe door
The one-line rule. Every habit must be pre-loaded in the physical space where the anchor happens. If it isn't visible, it isn't chosen.
For the environment-first framework, the classic read is Charles Duhigg, The Power of Habit — amazon.fr/dp/081298160X.
08 · Identity Test

The 90-day identity test

Most people quit at day 30 because nothing felt different. Try this instead. Day 90, ask:

The three questions

1. Has the sentence "I'm someone who ___" changed for me? What did I believe 90 days ago vs. now?

2. What do people around me now see that they didn't 90 days ago?

3. If I had to keep only one of the three stacks I built — which one stays?

Interpretation. If question 1 has an honest answer, the stack worked. If it doesn't, the stack was too big or the anchor was weak. Shrink, re-anchor, restart.

09 · Failure Modes

Common ways this breaks

"I forgot three days in a row." → Anchor was too passive. Move the stack to a more physical anchor (opening a door, sitting down, brushing teeth — not "when I feel like it").
"The stack felt stupid." → Habit was too big. Cut it to 30 seconds. One pushup. One word written. Do that for a week, then grow.
"I did the habit but nothing changed." → Identity clause wasn't written. Re-read the sentence "so that I become ___" out loud every morning for 30 days. Habits compound in identity, not in outcomes.
"I got sick and stopped." → Restart with the smallest possible version. Never miss twice in a row after a restart; that's the actual rule.
"Traveling broke the anchor." → Design a travel version of every stack: 2 squats instead of 10; one sentence in phone Notes instead of journal. Anchor stays, habit scales down.
"I tried 5 stacks at once." → You're not building habits. You're doing a self-improvement project. Cap at 3. One becomes automatic before adding the fourth.
For the recovery protocol after a break, Dave Asprey-adjacent is not the answer. The right book is B.J. Fogg, Tiny Habits — amazon.fr/dp/059323484X.

12 · Where This Goes

After the kit

The Habit Stack Kit is step one. If you finished 30 days, your next move depends on what actually stuck:

  1. Anchor is automatic, stack still feels forced. → Shrink the stack to 30 seconds and run 30 more days. Do not grow it yet.
  2. Stack is automatic, but you didn't get the outcome. → Wrong identity clause. Rewrite it. "I'm someone who writes" is different from "I'm someone who writes to think clearly."
  3. Stack is automatic and you got the outcome. → Add the fourth habit only after 30 more days of the three. Slow is fast.
