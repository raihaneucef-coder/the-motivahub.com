  - q: "Is working memory the same as short-term memory?"
    a: "They overlap, but they are not identical. Short-term memory is the brief holding of information. Working memory also includes manipulating that information and controlling attention while you do it. Remembering a phone number for a few seconds is short-term memory; reversing it while ordering a list is working memory."
  - q: "Why do small tasks feel so overwhelming?"
    a: "The size of the task is only one part of the load. Uncertainty, interruptions, unfinished tasks, sleep loss and stress all compete for the same limited workspace. A task can be small and still feel heavy if you are holding five other things at the same time."

    a: "They usually make you better at the trained game. The problem is transfer: the improvement often does not carry over to unrelated tasks such as reading, planning or decision-making. Treat them as practice at a specific task, not as a general cognitive upgrade."

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p1"><p>Working memory is the small desk of the mind.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p2"><p>It is where you hold the sentence you just read while you decide what it means, the number you are carrying from one step to the next, the name of the person you just met, and the next action in a task. It is not a warehouse for everything you know. It is the active workspace you use to think.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p3"><p>That distinction matters because most people describe mental overload as a personal failure: <em>I should be able to keep track of this.</em> Sometimes the problem is not effort. It is the number of things competing for a workspace that is genuinely small.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p4"><h2>What working memory actually is</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p5"><p>Baddeley and Hitch (1974) proposed a multicomponent model of working memory. Their original version included a central executive that coordinates attention, a phonological loop for verbal information, and a visuospatial sketchpad for images and locations. Later versions added an episodic buffer that integrates different kinds of information.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p6"><p>You do not need the full diagram to use the idea. The point is that working memory is a bottleneck, not a storage unit. It holds what you are working with <em>right now</em>, and it has to be continuously updated as the task changes.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p7"><p>Cowan (2001) reviewed the evidence and argued that the core capacity is closer to <strong>four chunks</strong> than to the old 7±2 slogan. The exact number is still debated, and it depends on how you measure it. The practical implication is more stable than the number: when a task asks you to hold too many separate elements at once, performance falls.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p8"><p>A chunk is not a single letter or number. It is a meaningful unit: a word, a phrase, a step, a pattern. This is why expertise changes what fits. An experienced editor can hold the shape of a paragraph while a beginner is still decoding the first sentence. The editor's working memory did not magically grow. The unit became larger.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p9"><h2>Why the muscle metaphor fails</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p10"><p>The muscle metaphor is attractive because it suggests a simple plan: train harder, get stronger. But working memory is better understood as a capacity that can be supported or overloaded. You can become more skilled at a particular task, and that skill changes how much information fits into a chunk. You can also learn strategies that reduce the load. That is not the same as permanently enlarging the workspace for everything you do.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p11"><p>This is why two people can face the same task and feel completely different. One has a clear next step, a written list and no interruptions. The other is holding five unfinished tasks, three tabs, an unanswered message and a deadline. The second person is not necessarily less disciplined. They are carrying more.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p12"><h2>What the training evidence actually says</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p13"><p>Brain-training products usually make a familiar promise: train working memory, and attention or intelligence will improve too. The evidence is more modest.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p14"><p>Simons and colleagues (2016) reviewed the cognitive-training literature and found that people improve on the tasks they practise. Near transfer — improvement on a similar task — is limited. Far transfer — improvement on unrelated tasks or general intelligence — is weak. Sala and Gobet (2019) reached a similar conclusion: cognitive training does not reliably enhance general cognition.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p15"><p>A 2024 meta-analysis by Rodas, Asimakopoulou and Greene analysed 52 independent comparisons. It found a small average improvement on working-memory measures (SMD = 0.18). When the assessment task closely resembled the trained task, the effect was much larger (SMD = 1.15). Fluid intelligence did not improve, and improvements in working memory were not related to changes in fluid intelligence. Some specific executive functions did improve, but the broad upgrade promised by commercial programmes was not there.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p16"><p>Another 2024 review, a second-order meta-analysis by Syed and colleagues, aggregated six meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials with healthy adults. It estimated a small average improvement in working memory (SMD = 0.335). It did not find one training type clearly superior to another, and the authors noted that the effect was small enough that real-life utility may be minimal.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p17"><p>The honest translation: practise the task, get better at the task. Do not assume the gain follows you into email, meetings, planning and decisions. This does not make training useless. It makes the marketing claim too large.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p18"><h2>What actually helps</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p19"><p>The useful question is not <em>How do I enlarge working memory?</em> It is <em>How do I reduce what working memory has to carry?</em> That question is more practical, and the evidence is stronger.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p20"><h3>1. Externalise the open loops</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p21"><p>An unfinished task keeps demanding attention. You may not be consciously thinking about it, but part of your mind is still rehearsing it: <em>Did I reply? Did I send that? What was the next step?</em></p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p22"><p>Give every open loop one home. A notebook, a notes app, a single list — the tool matters less than the rule: if it is unfinished, it goes there. Then choose one next action for the task you are about to do. Not the whole project. The next physical or mental step.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p23"><p>This is not a productivity ritual for its own sake. It is a way of moving information out of the limited workspace and into the environment, where it can wait without consuming attention (Masicampo &amp; Baumeister, 2011).</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p24"><h3>2. Reduce switching</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p25"><p>Every switch has a cost. Part of the cost is the seconds it takes to change task. The larger cost is reloading the problem: where you were, what you were about to do, what you had already ruled out (Leroy, 2009).</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p26"><p>One tab. One task. One short block. Put the phone in another room, not just face down on the desk. Close the applications you are not using. If a new task appears, write it down and return to it later. The goal is not perfect focus. The goal is fewer reloads.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p27"><p>This is the same principle behind <a href="/journal/deep-work-focus/">deep work</a>: the value comes from continuity, not from sitting at the desk for a long time while switching every few minutes.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p28"><h3>3. Chunk and scaffold</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p29"><p>Cognitive load theory explains why a messy interface, unclear instructions or too many examples at once can consume the same capacity you need for the task itself (Sweller, 1988; Sweller, van Merriënboer &amp; Paas, 2019). Paas and van Merriënboer (2020) describe practical ways to manage that load: use worked examples, remove unnecessary information, and give learners support that can be faded as competence grows.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p30"><p>In daily life, that looks like:</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p31"><ul>
<li>turning a vague project into three visible steps;</li>
<li>using a checklist so you do not have to remember the sequence;</li>
<li>writing the first sentence of the next section before you stop;</li>
<li>using a template for repetitive work;</li>
<li>asking for an example when instructions are abstract.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p32"><p>Chunking works because it changes the unit. Ten separate facts become one meaningful pattern. A report becomes four headings. A difficult conversation becomes three points. The capacity is still limited, but the load is organised.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p33"><h3>4. Protect sleep</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p34"><p>Sleep loss is a load multiplier. A meta-analysis of 70 articles comprising 147 cognitive tests found that short-term total sleep deprivation impaired working-memory accuracy (g = −0.56) and increased lapses in simple attention (g = −0.78) (Lim &amp; Dinges, 2010). The included studies were limited to less than 48 hours of total sleep deprivation, so this is not a study of chronic partial sleep restriction. It still tells you something important: when you are sleep-deprived, the workspace is not operating at its normal level.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p35"><p>This does not mean that a perfect sleep schedule will solve an unreasonable workload. It means that sleep is part of the system you are asking to perform. If you are trying to compensate for poor sleep with more effort, you are usually making the task harder.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p36"><p>The practical version is unglamorous: a consistent wake time, a dark room, and a wind-down that does not end with a screen in your face. <a href="/journal/sleep-is-unfair-advantage/">Sleep is not a luxury</a>; it is an input.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p37"><h3>5. Use movement strategically</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p38"><p>A 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis by Garrett and colleagues combined 651 effect sizes from 113 studies and 4,390 young adults. It found a small beneficial effect of acute exercise on cognition (g = 0.13). When the analysis was restricted to executive-function tasks, working memory and inhibition improved. The effect was real, but modest.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p39"><p>For older adults, a 2021 meta-analysis by Cai and colleagues included 28 randomized controlled trials and 2,156 participants. It found a small-to-moderate effect of physical exercise on working memory (SMD = 0.30). The review's suggested protocol was multicomponent or mind-body exercise of moderate intensity, 45–60 minutes, about three times a week, for more than six months. The optimal prescription is still uncertain, and the studies varied.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p40"><p>What does this mean in practice? A short walk is not a cognitive upgrade. It is a modest reset. If you have been staring at the same sentence for twenty minutes, ten minutes of movement may do more for your next attempt than another twenty minutes of forcing it. Do not expect the walk to enlarge your capacity. Expect it to change your state.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p41"><h3>6. Design the environment</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p42"><p>The environment is doing part of your thinking, whether you notice or not. A phone on the desk, a crowded browser, a messy inbox and a list of unread messages all compete for the same workspace.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p43"><p>Make the right action the easy one:</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p44"><ul>
<li>phone in another room during a focused block;</li>
<li>one browser window for the current task;</li>
<li>a visible list of the three things that matter today;</li>
<li>a place for paper, notes and the next action;</li>
<li>fewer decisions before the work starts.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p45"><p>This is not about aesthetic minimalism. It is about reducing the number of things that can pull attention away from the thing you chose.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p46"><h2>A seven-day experiment</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p47"><p>This is not a clinical test. It is a personal experiment, and your results will be yours. The point is to gather a little evidence instead of guessing.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p48"><p><strong>Day 1 — Baseline.</strong> Three times during the day — morning, after lunch, end of day — rate how overloaded your head feels from 1 to 10. Note how many hours you slept, how many interruptions you had, and what you were trying to hold in your head.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p49"><p><strong>Day 2 — Capture.</strong> Keep one list for every unfinished task. Do not sort it. Do not prioritise it yet. Just get it out of your head and onto paper or into a notes app.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p50"><p><strong>Day 3 — One-tab block.</strong> Choose one task and give it 45 minutes. One tab, one document, phone in another room. If something else appears, write it on the capture list and return.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p51"><p><strong>Day 4 — Sleep anchor.</strong> Keep your wake time fixed, and give yourself a 30-minute wind-down without a screen before bed. Do not try to fix your whole sleep schedule in one night. Just create one anchor.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p52"><p><strong>Day 5 — Move before the hard thing.</strong> Take a 10-minute walk before the task you are most likely to avoid. Then start the task immediately, while the effect is fresh. The evidence for acute exercise is modest; the point is to test whether it helps <em>you</em> begin.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p53"><p><strong>Day 6 — Chunk one task.</strong> Take the task you have been avoiding and break it into three visible steps. Write the first step as an action you could start in two minutes. Use a checklist or a worked example if the task is unfamiliar.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p54"><p><strong>Day 7 — Review.</strong> Compare your overload ratings with Day 1. Which change made the biggest difference? Which one are you willing to keep for another week? Keep one change, not seven.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p55"><p>If the experiment makes one thing easier, that is useful data. If nothing changes, that is also useful: it may mean the load is coming from somewhere else — sleep, health, a workload that is genuinely too large, or a problem that needs proper support.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p56"><h2>What this will not fix</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p57"><p>The evidence on working-memory training is noisy. Effect sizes depend on the tasks, the samples, the comparison groups and the way the training is measured. Exercise and sleep studies use different doses, populations and outcome measures. A meta-analysis can tell you the average effect across many people; it cannot tell you what will happen to you.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p58"><p>This article also cannot diagnose you. Persistent difficulty with attention, memory or organisation can have many causes, including sleep problems, anxiety, depression, ADHD, medication effects or other health issues. If the problem is affecting your work, relationships or daily life, talk to a qualified professional. A seven-day experiment is not a substitute for care.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p59"><p>What the evidence does support is a simpler claim: you do not need a bigger working memory to feel less overloaded. You need fewer things competing for the one you have. That is not a motivational slogan. It is a design problem.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p60"><h2>The honest summary</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p61"><p>Working memory is small. It is not a moral failing, and it is not a muscle you can flex into infinity. The training evidence shows that you can get better at trained tasks, but broad transfer is weak. The practical gains come from reducing load: externalise open loops, cut switching, chunk the task, protect sleep, use movement, and design the environment.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_working-memory-not-a-muscle_p62"><p>Then test it for a week. Not to prove that you are disciplined. To find out what actually makes the work easier to carry.</p></div>
