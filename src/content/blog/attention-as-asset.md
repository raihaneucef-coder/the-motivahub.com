# Attention as Asset

Your attention is the only thing the modern economy cannot manufacture more of. Treat it like the finite, appreciating asset it is.

I learned this the hard way in 2023. I was working on my first book, and I thought I was being productive. I had my laptop open, my phone on the desk, three tabs in the browser, and a Slack notification every 5 minutes.

At the end of the day, I had 400 words. Four hundred. It took me 8 hours to write 400 words. That is 50 words per hour. A trained typist types 40 words per minute. I was slower than a typist who types with one finger.

The problem was not my writing ability. The problem was my attention. It was being stolen, in small amounts, every few minutes.

## The Most Expensive Commodity

Factories can print money. They cannot print attention. Every waking hour of your life, your attention is being harvested, packaged, and sold — by the newsfeed, the notification, the advertisement, the endless scroll.

The Stoics called this *the theft of the self*. Seneca wrote: "It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it." Two thousand years later, the waste has been industrialised — and you are the raw material.

In 2023, I tracked my attention for one day. I discovered that I spent:
- **3.5 hours** on my phone (mostly scrolling)
- **1.5 hours** on email
- **1 hour** on Slack/notifications
- **2 hours** on "deep work" (but with constant interruptions)
- **2 hours** on meetings

Total productive attention: 2 hours out of 16 waking hours. That is 12.5% efficiency. The rest was stolen.

## What Attention Actually Is

Attention is the ability to have one thing in your mind at a time. It is the faculty that turns raw time into work, love, and understanding. Without attention, an hour is empty. With attention, an hour is a life.

The modern world is engineered against it. Every app is designed to break attention into smaller pieces — because a broken attention is a piece of merchandise. The fragment is the sale.

The average person checks their phone 96 times per day. That is once every 10 waking minutes. Each check is a small theft. Each notification is a small hijacking. The fragments add up to hours.

## The Account Ledger

Think of your attention as a bank account with a daily deposit. Every day you receive roughly sixteen waking hours. The question is not how much you have. The question is who withdraws.

- The phone: 14 withdrawals a day, minimum
- The news: another handful, often involuntary
- The endless conversation that could have been an email: one large withdrawal
- The hour of deep work: one massive deposit — the only thing that makes you richer

The disciplined mind is not the one with more attention. It is the one that audits the ledger.

In 2023, my ledger looked like this:
- Withdrawals: 14 hours (phone, email, Slack, meetings)
- Deposits: 2 hours (deep work)
- Net: -12 hours

I was bankrupt. Not financially. Attentionaly.

## The Audit

Take one day and count. Every time you reach for the phone, mark it. Every notification that pulls your eyes from the task, mark it. Every conversation, every tab, every thought that wanders — mark it.

You will be shocked by the invoice. Most people discover that the majority of their attention is not spent — it is stolen, in small amounts, by machines designed for the theft.

I did this audit in March 2023. I used a simple notebook. Every time I checked my phone, I made a mark. Every time I switched tabs, I made a mark. Every time I got a notification, I made a mark.

At the end of the day, I had 147 marks. 147 thefts. Some were 2 seconds. Some were 10 minutes. The total time stolen: 3.5 hours.

The audit was painful. But it was also liberating. Once I saw the theft, I could stop it.

## How to Protect the Asset

### 1. The Fixed Do Not Disturb

Two hours a day, the phone is in another room. Not silent — elsewhere. The Stoics understood the power of *prohairesis*: the choice made before the moment. Remove the option, and the choice is already won.

In 2023, I started doing this from 6:30 AM to 8:30 AM. The phone stayed in the kitchen. I worked in my home office. No notifications. No calls. No texts. Just me and the writing.

The first week was painful. I kept reaching for the phone. I kept imagining emergencies. Nothing happened. No one died. No one got angry. The world continued without my attention.

By week 3, I had doubled my word count. From 400 words per day to 800. By week 6, I was at 1,200 words per day. The phone in another room was the unlock.

### 2. The Single Tab

One tab. One task. One window. The multitasker is not doing more — they are paying a switching tax on every task, and the tax compounds.

Research from the University of Michigan found that multitasking can reduce productivity by up to 40%. The switching cost compounds with every task change. Single-tasking is not just better. It is the only sustainable way to work.

In 2023, I started using a single-tab browser for writing. One document. No email. No Slack. No research. Just writing. When I needed to research, I opened a new window. When I was done, I closed it. The single tab forced me to finish one thing before starting another.

### 3. The Attention Budget

You have 16 waking hours. Budget them like money.

- 2 hours: deep work (the deposit)
- 2 hours: shallow work (email, admin)
- 2 hours: meetings
- 2 hours: learning
- 4 hours: rest, family, leisure
- 2 hours: buffer (for the unexpected)

The budget is not rigid. It is a guide. The point is to be intentional about where your attention goes, not to let it be stolen by whatever pulls at it.

In 2023, I started tracking my attention budget in a notebook. Every hour, I marked what I had done. At the end of the week, I reviewed the ledger. Where had I overspent? Where had I underspent? What needed to change?

### 4. The Notification Fast

Notifications are not helpful. They are interruptions dressed up as help. Every notification is a small theft of attention.

In 2023, I turned off all notifications except phone calls from my wife. No email notifications. No Slack notifications. No social media notifications. No news notifications.

The first week was quiet. Too quiet. I felt like I was missing something. By week 2, I had forgotten what the notifications felt like. By week 3, I had reclaimed 1.5 hours per day.

## The Relationship Between Attention and Identity

The deepest part of the system is the identity statement. Without it, the rule is a behavior with no destination. With it, the rule is a vote in an election.

"I am a writer who publishes" is the destination. The 2-minute rule of writing one sentence is the vote. The 2-day rule is the protection of the vote. The attention budget is the consequence of the vote being true.

When the system is working, the identity statement becomes more true over time. The statement is no longer aspirational. It is descriptive. You are a writer who publishes. You are a runner who races. You are a reader who finishes books. The votes have made it so.

When the system is not working, the identity statement remains aspirational. You say "I am a writer who publishes" but you have not published in three months. The statement is a wish, not a fact. The votes have not been cast.

Attention is what makes the votes possible. Without attention, the votes are scattered. With attention, the votes accumulate.

## What I Got Wrong (And How I Fixed It)

I made three big mistakes:

**Mistake 1: Thinking I was busy**
I thought 8 hours at the computer meant 8 hours of work. It did not. It meant 8 hours of attention theft. The fix: track the actual productive hours, not the time spent at the computer.

**Mistake 2: Believing in multitasking**
I thought I could write and check email at the same time. I could not. The switching tax cost me 40% of my productivity. The fix: single-tab, single-task work.

**Mistake 3: Ignoring the ledger**
I never tracked where my attention went. The theft was invisible. The audit made it visible. Once I saw the theft, I could stop it.

## A Simple Test for Your Week

If you want to test this for yourself, here is the protocol:

1. **Pick one day this week.**
2. **Track every attention theft:** phone check, notification, tab switch, wandering thought.
3. **Count the marks.**
4. **Calculate the total time stolen.**
5. **Implement one rule:** phone in another room during deep work.
6. **Track the difference.**

Most people who do this for one week report:
- 2-3 hours of recovered attention
- 2x increase in deep work output
- Lower stress and anxiety
- More presence in conversations

The data will convince you faster than any productivity book.
