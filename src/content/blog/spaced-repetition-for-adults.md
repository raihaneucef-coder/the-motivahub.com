<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p1"><p>You <a href="/journal/how-to-read-30-books-a-year/">read something once</a>. It makes sense. You close the book. A week later, it is gone.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p2"><p>The fix is not to read more times in a row. It is to spread the reviews out. That is spaced repetition: revisiting material after increasing intervals, ideally by trying to recall it before you look.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p3"><h2>Spacing is not the same as rereading</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p4"><p>Rereading can create familiarity. Familiarity feels like knowledge, but it is not the same as being able to retrieve the information later. Retrieval practice — testing yourself — is a separate ingredient. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found that students who practiced retrieval remembered more later than students who restudied, even when the restudy group felt more confident in the moment.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p5"><p>Spacing and retrieval work well together, but they are not interchangeable. You can space rereading, and it will still be weaker than spaced retrieval. That is why the schedule below asks you to recall first, then check.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p6"><h2>What the evidence says</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p7"><p>The spacing effect is one of the more robust findings in learning research. Cepeda et al. (2006) reviewed 839 assessments from 317 experiments in 184 articles and found that distributed practice generally beat massed practice for verbal recall. The optimal gap was not one fixed number; it depended on how long you needed to remember the material.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p8"><p>Cepeda et al. (2008) tested more than 1,350 participants and found a similar pattern: the best spacing interval scales with the retention interval. For a test about one week later, the optimal gap was roughly 20–40% of that delay. For a test about one year later, it was roughly 5–10% of the delay. In plain terms: the longer you need the knowledge, the longer the gaps should be.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p9"><p>Kim and Webb (2022) meta-analyzed 98 effect sizes from 48 experiments with 3,411 participants in second-language learning. Spacing produced a medium effect immediately (g = 0.58) and a larger effect on delayed tests (g = 0.80). Longer spacing was better than shorter spacing (g = 0.40), and equal intervals and expanding intervals were statistically equivalent overall. That last point matters: you do not need a perfect algorithm to benefit.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p10"><p>Kang (2016) summarizes the practical case: spaced repetition is efficient because it fights forgetting, and it is effective when it includes retrieval. The evidence is strongest for facts, vocabulary, definitions, and other material that can be tested. For complex skills, spacing helps, but it does not replace practice with feedback.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p11"><h2>A five-review schedule</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p12"><p>Here is a simple schedule for a fact, a definition, a vocabulary item, or a short set of notes you need to keep. It uses five reviews after the first learning session:</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p13"><ul>
<li>Day 0: learn the material.</li>
<li>Day 1: first review.</li>
<li>Day 3: second review.</li>
<li>Day 7: third review.</li>
<li>Day 21: fourth review — scheduled for the future, not done on Day 0.</li>
<li>Day 60: fifth review — scheduled for the future, not done on Day 0.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p14"><p>At each review:</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p15"><ol>
<li>Close the notes.</li>
<li>Write or say what you remember.</li>
<li>Check what you missed.</li>
<li>Correct the gap.</li>
<li>Stop. Do not keep rereading until it feels smooth.</li>
</ol></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p16"><p><a href="/journal/2-minute-rule-system/">The first review is quick</a>. The later reviews are also quick if you have been retrieving. If a review takes a long time, the material may be too large; split it into smaller items.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p17"><h2>Why this schedule is only a starting point</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p18"><p>The “right” interval depends on the material and on when you need it. If you need the information for years, stretch the gaps. If you need it for an exam next week, compress them. The schedule above is a reasonable default for general adult learning, not a tested protocol or a law.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p19"><p>Also, spacing is not a substitute for understanding. If the material is confusing, more spacing will just give you more opportunities to practice confusion. Learn it well enough to explain it, then space the reviews.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p20"><h2>Common mistakes</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p21"><ul>
<li><strong>Only rereading.</strong> Rereading can feel productive, but it is a weak test of whether you can retrieve the information. Recall first.</li>
<li><strong>Cramming everything into one session.</strong> Concentrating all practice in one block — massed practice — often produces fast gains and fast forgetting. Spread it out.</li>
<li><strong>Making the reviews too big.</strong> A review should be a retrieval attempt, not a reread of the whole chapter. Use small items.</li>
<li><strong>Skipping the later reviews.</strong> Day 21 and Day 60 are easy to ignore because they are far away. Put them in the calendar now.</li>
<li><strong>Chasing the perfect algorithm.</strong> Equal and expanding intervals performed similarly in the Kim and Webb meta-analysis. Consistency matters more than optimization.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p22"><h2>How to use it for different material</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p23"><p><strong>Vocabulary and definitions:</strong> make a question on one side and the answer on the other. Retrieve, check, repeat.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p24"><p><strong>Concepts from a book:</strong> write a short prompt for each idea. At each review, answer from memory, then check the book.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p25"><p><strong>Procedures:</strong> spacing helps you remember the steps, but you also need to perform the procedure. Alternate between recall and actual practice.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p26"><p><strong>Complex skills:</strong> use spacing for the knowledge around the skill, and deliberate practice for the skill itself. Feedback is not optional.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p27"><h2>What it cannot do</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p28"><p>Spaced repetition is not a cure for poor instruction, and it does not make up for missing practice. It also does not guarantee that you will understand something; it helps you keep what you have learned. And the effect sizes from second-language research should not be treated as universal numbers for every domain. The direction is reliable; the exact size depends on the task, the learner, and the delay.</p></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p29"><h2>A two-month trial</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p30"><ol>
<li>Choose ten items you actually need to remember.</li>
<li>Write one prompt for each.</li>
<li>Schedule reviews for Day 1, Day 3, Day 7, Day 21, and Day 60.</li>
<li>At each review, retrieve first, then check.</li>
<li>Track two numbers: how many you recalled, and how many you corrected.</li>
<li>After Day 60, adjust the item size, not the whole system.</li>
</ol></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_spaced-repetition-for-adults_p31"><p>The goal is not to <a href="/journal/slow-productivity-30-day-test/">review everything</a>. It is to review the right things at the right intervals, with enough effort to make the memory work.</p></div>
