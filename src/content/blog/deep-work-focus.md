faq:
  - q: "What is deep work in simple terms?"
    a: "Work that is mentally hard and done without interruption. Two conditions have to hold at the same time: the task stretches your thinking, and nothing is pulling your attention away. Writing, designing, analysing and building qualify; answering email while skimming a report does not."
  - q: "How many hours of deep work per day is realistic?"
    a: "For most people, one to two hours. The deliberate-practice research behind the idea found even elite performers topping out around four hours a day, in sessions of about an hour. If you are new to it, a single honest 60-90 minute block beats a planned four."
  - q: "How long should a deep work session be?"
    a: "Start at 25 minutes and work up to 90. Under about 15 minutes you never get past re-loading the problem, so the session produces nothing; past roughly 90 minutes most people's attention decays faster than the fatigue they are banking. A 90-minute block with a five-minute walk after it is the standard unit."
  - q: "Is deep work the same as flow?"
    a: "No. Flow is the state where the work feels effortless and self-reinforcing, and it usually arrives only after you have been engaged with a task that matches your skill. Deep work includes the awkward first ten minutes, the hard revision and the parts that never feel good. You want flow, but the definition of a successful session is what got finished, not how it felt."

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p3">Cal Newport defined deep work as "professional activities performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that push your cognitive capabilities to their limit." It is the skill that separates the best from the rest.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p18"><h2>What Deep Work Actually Means</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p19">Newport's definition has two halves, and most people only notice the second one. Deep work is (1) a cognitively demanding task and (2) done without distraction. The first half is about the work; the second is about you. A task can be hard and still be done shallowly, if you do it while half-watching a screen.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p20">Three questions sort almost any task correctly:
<ul><li><strong>Does it create new value, or only keep something running?</strong> Writing the plan is deep; forwarding it is not.</li><li><strong>Could someone with a third of your skill do it after a week of training?</strong> If yes, it is probably shallow work that pays a senior salary.</li><li><strong>Does it break if you interrupt it every four minutes?</strong> If yes, it needs depth, and your calendar has to say so.</li></ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p21">The reason the distinction matters is not moral. Shallow work feels productive while you are doing it — the inbox empties, the replies come in, the day looks full. Deep work feels uncomfortable at the start and leaves a different residue at the end: you can point to the one thing that exists now and did not exist this morning.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p5"><h2>Why Deep Work Matters</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p6">The economy rewards two types of people: those who can work with intelligent machines, and those who are the best at what they do. Both require deep work.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p7">Shallow work — emails, meetings, administrative tasks — keeps you busy. Deep work creates value. The problem is that most people spend all day in shallow mode and wonder why they are not progressing.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p22"><h2>Choose Your Scheduling Philosophy</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p23">Before any technique, decide how deep work will sit in your week. Newport describes four philosophies, and the useful part is that they are alternatives, not a ladder. <strong>Monastic:</strong> long sealed-off stretches, remote from communication — for people whose output is one long artifact. <strong>Bimodal:</strong> days or weeks of isolation alternating with normal, reachable time — the realistic version for anyone with a job. <strong>Rhythmic:</strong> the same block every day, at the same hour — the easiest to sustain and the one most people should start with. <strong>Journalistic:</strong> deep work whenever a window opens — powerful, exhausting, and nearly impossible until focus has become automatic.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p37">If you have a normal working life, choose rhythmic. A rule that depends on finding a free week will not run twice a month; a rule that depends on 07:00 to 08:30 will.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p8"><h2>How to Build Deep Work</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p9"><h3>1. Schedule It</h3>
Block 2-4 hours every day for deep work. Put it in your calendar. Treat it like a meeting with your most important client — because your most important client is your future self.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p10"><h3>2. Eliminate Distractions</h3>
Phone on airplane mode. All notifications off. No email. No social media. No interruptions. If you cannot eliminate all distractions, eliminate as many as possible.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p11"><h3>3. Start Small</h3>
If you have never done deep work, start with 25 minutes (one Pomodoro). Then increase to 45 minutes. Then 90 minutes. Your focus muscle needs training.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p24"><h2>Inside a Deep Work Session</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p25">A session has three parts, and the first ten minutes are always the worst. Plan for them instead of being surprised by them.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p26"><strong>The opening.</strong> Write one sentence naming the single thing that will be true when the block ends — "a decision about the pricing page", not "work on the site". Then write the exit note: the exact next action you will take when you resume. Stopping with a known next sentence is Hemingway's rule, and it removes the worst part of a session, which is starting tomorrow's.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p27"><strong>The middle.</strong> One question, one tab, timer visible. When an unrelated but legitimate task appears — the invoice, the reply you should send — it goes on a list beside you, not into the browser. Each interruption does not cost the two minutes it takes; it costs the several minutes it takes your attention to come back, which is why a session with six "quick" checks feels finished but produced nothing.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p28"><strong>The close.</strong> Five minutes, no screen: what did I finish, what is the next action, when does this block happen tomorrow? Then an explicit shutdown. Newport's "shutdown complete" sounds theatrical until you notice how much of your evening was spent half-thinking about unresolved work. A written ending closes the loop and returns processing power you were paying for all day.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p29"><h2>Measure Depth, Not Hours</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p30">The useful unit is not the hour but the hour-times-concentration. Six hours spent checking a phone every few minutes is not six hours of deep work; it is six hours of shallow work with a serious book on the desk. Three honest hours will usually beat them.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p31">There is also a ceiling. The performance research Newport builds on — Anders Ericsson's studies of deliberate practice — found that even elite performers sustained roughly four hours a day at most, in sessions around an hour long. If your plan needs six unbroken hours, the plan is the problem, not your discipline.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p32"><h2>Why Deep Work Attempts Collapse</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p33"><ol><li><strong>The goal is too vague to be deep.</strong> "Work on the project" cannot be finished, so the mind drifts and the block becomes browsing with intent.</li><li><strong>Deep work is scheduled after the inbox.</strong> By 11:30 your capacity for sustained attention has been spent on other people's priorities. The block has to go first, before willpower is gone.</li><li><strong>The phone is in the room.</strong> Research on mere presence has found that a visible phone reduces available cognitive capacity even when switched off. Put it in another room; "face down on the desk" is not a solution.</li><li><strong>Nothing is written at the end.</strong> Without a daily line — hours, depth, next action — a bad week and a good week look identical, so the habit never gets corrected.</li></ol></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p13"><h2>The Deep Work Schedule</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_deep-work-focus_p14">The best time for deep work is the morning, when your willpower is highest. Protect your morning for deep work. Save shallow work for the afternoon.</div>
