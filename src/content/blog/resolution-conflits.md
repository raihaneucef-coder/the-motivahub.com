<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p2">Every relationship has conflict. The question is not whether you will fight — it is how. Learn to fight fairly and strengthen your bond.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p3">Conflict is not the enemy of relationships. Contempt is. When you fight fairly, conflict can actually strengthen your bond. When you fight unfairly, it destroys trust.</div>


<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p4"><h2>The 4 Horsemen of Relationship Apocalypse</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p5"><h3>1. Criticism</h3>
Attacking the person instead of the behavior. "You never help" is criticism. "I need more help with dishes" is a complaint.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p6"><h3>2. Contempt</h3>
Treating the other person with disrespect. Sarcasm, mockery, eye-rolling, and name-calling are all contempt. It is the single greatest predictor of divorce.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p7"><h3>3. Defensiveness</h3>
Deflecting responsibility. "It's not my fault" or "You do it too" prevents resolution.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p8"><h3>4. Stonewalling</h3>
Shutting down. Walking away. Giving the silent treatment. This is the relationship killer.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p9"><h2>How to Fight Fair</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p10"><h3>1. <a href="/journal/love-is-a-practice/">Use "I" Statements</a></h3>
Gottman calls the antidote to criticism a <em>soft start-up</em>: describe the feeling, name the specific situation, then state one concrete need — and skip the word "always." "I felt alone when I cooked alone tonight; I'd love twenty minutes together after dinner" lands differently than "You never help around here." Same complaint, but one invites repair and the other invites defence.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p11"><h3>2. Stay on Topic</h3>
Defensiveness escalates when an argument becomes a subpoena of past grievances. Hold one issue per conversation. If the older hurt is real, it gets its own time later — dragging it in now only lets both people lose the actual thread and arrive nowhere.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p12"><h3>3. Take Breaks</h3>
<a href="https://www.gottman.com/blog/flooding-and-conflict-avoidance/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Gottman's labs</a> found that when heart rate climbs past roughly 100 bpm during a fight, you're "flooded" — your thinking brain is essentially offline and you cannot process words. A 20-minute break <a href="/journal/gestion-energie/">resets your physiology</a> — it's long enough for the nervous system to come out of flood. The non-negotiable rule: say when you'll return ("I need 20 minutes, then I want to finish this") so a break isn't experienced as stonewalling.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p13"><h3>4. Listen to Understand</h3>
Your goal is not to win; it's to make the other person feel heard before you make yourself understood. The test is simple: you can't accurately restate their point to their satisfaction until you've actually taken it in. Repeat it back before you answer.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_resolution-conflits_p14"><h3>5. Repair Attempts</h3>
A repair attempt is any gesture — a joke, an admission, a soft tone, a touch — that lowers tension mid-argument. Gottman's 36-year data found the dividing line wasn't whether couples fight but whether they <em>accept</em> each other's repairs. Stable relationships run about five positive interactions for every one negative; the number is a summary of a habit, not a scoreboard to game.</div>
