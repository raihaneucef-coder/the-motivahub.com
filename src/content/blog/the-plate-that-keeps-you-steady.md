faq:
  - q: "What is the 'steady plate' method?"
    a: "Fill half your plate with vegetables, one quarter with protein (palm-sized), one quarter with slow carbs (rice, potato, whole grain), plus a thumb of healthy fat. The reason it works is satiety, not blood-sugar heroics: protein and fibre are what keep hunger quiet between meals (<a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926512/\">Leidy et al., Am J Clin Nutr 2015</a>), and a plate built this way is hard to overeat. Your body keeps glucose in its normal band either way — the plate is a portion and fullness tool, not a medical device."
  - q: "Why do I get energy crashes after lunch?"
    a: "Usually not for the insulin story the internet tells. Healthy bodies counter-regulate a mixed meal quickly, and a scoping review of food, glucose and workplace sleepiness found that what you eat does affect post-meal sleepiness while the direct evidence on glucose swings is still thin (Kaneda et al., <a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41156470/\">Nutrients 2025</a>). The bigger contributors are mundane: total portion size, a bad night, the early-afternoon dip in alertness your body clock runs anyway, and under-hydration (<a href=\"/journal/hydratation-performance/\">hydration and performance</a>). Protein, vegetables and a sensible portion take the hunger out of it, which is most of the battle."
  - q: "How much protein do I need per meal?"
    a: "A palm-sized portion (20-35g) at each main meal is the practical rule, and it lines up with the per-meal target used in the protein literature — roughly 0.4 g per kg of body weight. What you total across the day matters more than any single meal (see <a href=\"/journal/body-recomposition-guide/\">how much protein per day</a>); the plate method simply makes the target visible without a scale. Eating too little protein leaves you hungrier and protects less muscle while you lose fat."

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_h2a"><h2>Start with the plate</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p3">A steady day starts with a steady plate.</div>


<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p4">When your plate is mostly quick carbs, you are through it by ten o'clock and reaching for whatever is nearest. When it is balanced, the same meal still feels like dinner two hours later. This is not a diet. It is a ratio you can use for years — one of the habits detailed in <a href="/journal/fuel-before-willpower/">fuel before willpower</a>.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_h2b"><h2>The ratio: half, a quarter, a quarter</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p5"><strong>The steady plate is simple: half vegetables, a quarter protein, a quarter slow carbs, plus a thumb of fat.</strong></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p6">- <a href="/journal/meal-prep-dimanche/">Half vegetables</a>: fiber and color. They slow everything down in a good way.
- Quarter protein: keeps you full and protects muscle. Eggs, fish, beans, yogurt — what you like and can repeat.
- Quarter slow carbs: rice, potatoes, oats, bread that still looks like food.
- Thumb of fat: olive oil, nuts, avocado. Small, but it makes the meal satisfying.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_h2e"><h2>Why the ratio works</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p12">The physiology behind the plate rule, stated carefully: refined carbohydrate on an empty stomach empties fast and leaves you hungry again sooner, because nothing in it slowed digestion or signalled satiety. In healthy people the counter-regulatory system brings glucose back into its normal band, so the 11 a.m. shakiness and craving are better read as hunger and habit than as a glucose crash — the research on food, glucose and afternoon sleepiness still finds the mechanism thin while confirming that what you eat matters (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41156470/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Kaneda et al., Nutrients 2025</a>). Pairing the same carbohydrate with protein and fat slows gastric emptying and keeps appetite quiet, and that part of the story holds up.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p13">Practical swap examples: toast plus a boiled egg instead of toast alone. Oatmeal with a spoonful of nut butter instead of plain oats. An apple with a handful of almonds instead of the apple by itself. None of these require cooking skill or extra time; they just stop the hunger before it starts.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_h2c"><h2>No weighing, no counting</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p7">You do not need to weigh or count. Use your hand, your eye, and how you feel two hours later.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_h2d"><h2>Try it for three lunches</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p8">Try it for three lunches. Not perfectly, just steadily. Notice how the afternoon feels. That feeling is the information.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p10">The plate, concretely: half of what you eat at a sitting is vegetables or salad, a quarter protein, a quarter starch — and the vegetables get the first fork (<a href="https://www.hsph.harvard.edu/nutritionsource/healthy-eating-plate/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Harvard Healthy Eating Plate</a>). No weighing, no macro counting, no cleanse. The boring plate, done consistently, is the quiet rival of every aggressive diet that later needs a comeback article. And if the goal gets specific — losing fat while keeping the muscle you have already built — the plate is the starting point, not the whole plan: <a href="/journal/body-recomposition-guide/">the body recomposition guide</a> is the next step.</div>


<div data-i18n-html="article_the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady_p9">A steady plate does not require willpower. It creates it.</div>
