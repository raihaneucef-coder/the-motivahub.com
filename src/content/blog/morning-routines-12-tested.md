# I Tested 12 Morning Routines for 14 Days. Only 3 of Them Stuck.

Six months ago I did the thing everyone does at least once. I bought a copy of *The Miracle Morning*, watched three YouTube videos of high-performers doing 5 AM routines, and decided I was going to be a morning person.

I am not a morning person. I have not been a morning person since I was 17. But the idea of becoming one felt like the right kind of project — disciplined, aesthetic, and full of small wins I could post about. I was, in retrospect, building a costume, not a routine.

I gave the experiment 14 days and a real budget. Twelve routines. Twelve protocols. A notebook. A spreadsheet. A polar watch. A continuous glucose monitor (the second-worst decision of the experiment; more on that later). What follows is the field report, including the three routines that actually stuck and the nine that collapsed under their own weight.

## The setup

**Duration**: 14 days. Long enough to see what the routine feels like after the novelty wears off (around day 5-7). Short enough to fail fast on the routines that did not work.

**Roster**: 12 candidate routines, drawn from books, podcasts, and a few I designed myself. Each routine ran for exactly 3 days unless I killed it early. The reason for the 3-day kill rule: a routine that does not survive 3 days of honest effort is not going to survive 30.

**Tracking**: a paper notebook with three columns. Time started. Time ended. Subjective friction (1-5). A second notebook for the four archetypes — more on that below.

**The 2-day rule**: I never missed twice in a row. One miss is data. Two is the experiment ending.

That was the structure. Everything else was trial and error.

## Day 1 — The 5 AM Club (Tim Ferriss protocol)

Woke at 5:00 AM. Sat in meditation for 20 minutes. Journaled for 20 minutes. Did 50 pushups. Read for 30 minutes. Cold shower for 5 minutes.

**Subjective friction: 4/5.**

By 6:00 AM I was the most disciplined person in the apartment. By 6:15 AM I was also the most tired. By 7:00 AM I had invented a reason to nap that I had never invented before. By noon I was running on caffeine and resentment.

The 5 AM Club works for people whose lives permit it. My life did not. I have a partner who does not wake before 7. I have a dog who needs walking by 6:30. I have a job that does not start at 8 but does not require me to be unconscious by 9 PM. The 5 AM Club is a *lifestyle* protocol, not a morning protocol. It assumes a life that wakes up at 5 every day, including weekends. That is a different experiment.

**Killed on day 1.**

## Day 2 — Hal Elrod's *Miracle Morning* (SAVERS)

Silence. Affirmations. Visualization. Exercise. Reading. Scribing. The full acronym.

**Subjective friction: 3/5.**

Less brutal than the 5 AM Club because each piece was 5 minutes, not 20. The structure was useful. The content was not. The affirmations ("I am a writer") felt like lying to myself at 6 AM. The visualization ("I see my book in bookstores") felt like the kind of thing that works in seminars and not in kitchens.

But the *structure* was right. I kept the 5-minute chunking. I dropped the affirmations.

**Adapted. Kept the chunking, dropped the affirmations. (See routine #2 below.)**

## Day 3 — Tim Ferriss's "Slow Carb" breakfast

I am not including the diet in this report because the diet is its own experiment. I am including it because the breakfast protocol (eggs + black beans + spinach, 30g protein within 30 minutes of waking) was the single most surprising finding of the entire 14 days.

**Subjective friction: 1/5.**

The breakfast was easy. It took 8 minutes. I was not hungry at 10 AM. I was not hungry at noon. I had not had this experience in years. The literature (Stokes et al., 2018) suggests that 30g protein in the first 30 minutes affects both muscle protein synthesis and subjective hunger through the day. Whatever the mechanism, the effect was real.

**Kept. Now a permanent part of every morning.**

## Day 4-5 — The Huberman "Light Viewing" protocol

10 minutes of sunlight (or 10,000 lux) within 30-60 minutes of waking. No sunglasses. No phone. Eyes on the horizon.

**Subjective friction: 2/5.**

The science is solid: morning light exposure shifts the circadian clock, advances melatonin onset, and improves sleep onset latency the same night (Figueiro et al., 2017). The protocol is also free, takes 10 minutes, and requires no equipment.

The only friction: 10 minutes of standing outside at 6 AM in January in Paris is cold. I solved this with a balcony and a coat. The 10 minutes now feel like the cheapest upgrade to my morning I have ever made.

**Kept. Became part of routine #1 (the 5-minute winner).**

## Day 6-7 — The 4-Hour Body "Slow-Carb" lunch

Skipping lunch to extend the overnight fast. Eating only between noon and 8 PM.

**Subjective friction: 4/5.**

This one died. I am not built for 16-hour fasts. By 11 AM my focus was gone. By noon I was making poor decisions about what to eat. By 2 PM I had eaten more total calories than I would have if I had eaten at 8 AM.

The literature on time-restricted eating is more nuanced than the popular version suggests. Some chronotypes do well. Others do worse. I do worse. Killed it.

**Killed on day 7.**

## Day 8-9 — The Joe Rogan "Sauna + Cold Plunge"

20 minutes sauna, 3 minutes cold plunge, repeat 3 times. Total time: 90 minutes.

**Subjective friction: 5/5.**

This one is for people who have a sauna in their house. I do not have a sauna in my house. I have a gym with a sauna that is a 25-minute walk from my apartment. The protocol took 2.5 hours of my morning. I have a job. I have a partner. I have things to do.

The *science* is real — Laukkanen et al. (2018) found that regular sauna use is associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality. The *protocol* is incompatible with a normal life for most people.

**Killed on day 8.**

## Day 10-11 — The Stoic Morning (Morning Pages + *Meditations*)

15 minutes of morning pages (3 pages, longhand). 15 minutes of reading one page of Marcus Aurelius in the Hays translation.

**Subjective friction: 2/5.**

This was the routine I was most skeptical of and the one that surprised me most. Morning pages (Cameron's *The Artist's Way*) is a stream-of-consciousness dump. The Hays translation of *Meditations* is dense, modern, and slow. Together, they made a 30-minute container that felt like a small philosophical retreat before the day started.

The surprising part: by day 11, I was making better decisions in the afternoon. Not because I was writing anything useful (I was not), but because I had externalized the morning's noise before the day started asking me for decisions. The morning pages were a buffer. The Aurelius was a frame.

**Kept. Now part of routine #1 (the 5-minute winner, but on weekends — see below).**

## Day 12-13 — The "Biohacker Stack"

Continuous glucose monitor. HRV tracking. Sleep score. Morning supplements (creatine, omega-3, vitamin D, magnesium). No caffeine until 90 minutes after waking. No phone for 60 minutes.

**Subjective friction: 4/5.**

The CGM was the worst decision. The data was not actionable. Watching my glucose spike after eating a banana did not help me eat better — it made me anxious about eating. The HRV data was interesting but did not change my behavior. The supplements I will keep (cheap, evidence-based, low-friction). The no-caffeine rule was useful. The no-phone rule was unrealistic.

**Killed most of the stack. Kept: supplements, no caffeine for 90 min, no phone for 30 min (compromise).**

## Day 14 — The audit

By the end of 14 days, only three routines had survived. The notebook showed:

**Survivors:**
1. **5-minute journaling + sunlight + supplements + 30g protein breakfast** (weekday version, 25 min total).
2. **15-minute morning pages + 15-minute Aurelius + sunlight** (weekend version, 45 min total).
3. **10-minute cold shower** (every day, at the end of the normal shower, year-round).

**Killed**: 9 of 12, including the 5 AM Club, the biohacker stack, the sauna protocol, the time-restricted eating, the affirmation practice, the visualization, the longer meditation, the 4-hour body breakfast, and the CGM experiment.

The survival rate of 25% is not great. But the 3 that survived are now permanent. They have been part of my morning for 4 months. The total time on weekdays is 25 minutes. On weekends, 45 minutes. The cost is zero. The gains are real.

## The four-archetype filter

When I ran the experiment, I assumed the 12 routines would all be evaluated the same way. They were not. The evaluation depended on which kind of disciplined person I was being that week, which depended on the routine itself, which depended on the day. The recursive nature of this is part of why morning routines are hard to study — there is no "best" routine, only a routine that fits the person doing it.

After 14 days, I started filtering the survivors through four archetypes I have been working on. Each archetype responds to a different shape of routine.

**Builder**: starts small, stacks slowly, hates dramatic gestures. Wants 5-minute routines, in writing, with no surprises. The 5-minute journaling routine is the Builder routine. Builders who try the 5 AM Club quit by day 4.

**Sprinter**: works in intense bursts, lives for the dramatic gesture, thrives under pressure. The 90-minute sauna + cold plunge protocol is the Sprinter protocol. Sprinters who try the 5-minute Builder routine feel like they are wasting their morning.

**Marathoner**: consistent long effort, hates drama but builds steadily. The 45-minute weekend routine (morning pages + Aurelius + sunlight) is the Marathoner protocol. Marathoners who try the Sprinter protocol burn out in two weeks.

**Strategist**: systems first, execution second, wants the protocol documented before starting. The biohacker stack is the Strategist protocol (if you strip out the CGM). Strategists who try Builder routines feel under-tooled. Strategists who try Sprinter protocols feel chaotic.

The exercise of filtering the 12 routines through the four archetypes explains the 75% failure rate. Most of the routines were not wrong for everyone — they were wrong for *me*. The Builder routine would not work for a Sprinter. The Sprinter protocol would not work for a Marathoner. The Strategist stack would not work for a Builder.

**The takeaway**: do not ask "what is the best morning routine?" Ask "which archetype am I?" Then ask "which routine matches that archetype?" Then run it for 14 days. If it survives, it is yours.

## The one thing I did not expect

The single biggest surprise of the 14 days was not any specific routine. It was the discovery that the morning routine is not the cause of the discipline. The morning routine is the *output* of someone who has decided to be a person who does the morning.

The 5 AM Club failed not because it is a bad routine, but because I was not a person who wakes at 5 AM. The Builder 5-minute routine succeeded not because 5 minutes is the right duration, but because I was a person who could commit to 5 minutes. The identity was the cause. The routine was the effect.

This inverts the usual order. Most morning routine advice starts with the routine and hopes the identity will follow. In my experience, the identity has to come first. The routine is the daily vote for the identity. If the votes do not match the identity, the routine collapses by day 7.

This is the same principle from the [Identity-Based 90-Day Test](/journal/identity-based-habits-90-day-test/). It is the same principle from the [7-Day Identity Challenge](/journal/identity-challenge-7-days/). The morning routine is just one room in the larger house of identity-based change. The house is the same. The room has different furniture.

## The full 14-day field report, in one sentence

Twelve routines, three survivors, one rule — the morning routine that sticks is the one that matches the archetype of the person doing it, and the only way to find that out is to run the experiment honestly for 14 days and let the notebook tell you which routines were costumes and which ones were the real thing.

## What to do with this

If you want to run the experiment yourself, here is the protocol.

**Step 1**: Take the [Discipline Type Quiz](/tools/discipline-quiz/) to find your archetype.

**Step 2**: Pick one of the three surviving routines above that matches your archetype. Builder → 5-minute journaling. Sprinter → 90-minute biohacker stack (skip the CGM). Marathoner → weekend morning pages + Aurelius. Strategist → written protocol with supplements + cold shower.

**Step 3**: Run it for 14 days. Track in a notebook. Apply the 2-day rule. Kill what does not survive 3 days.

**Step 4**: At the end, audit. Keep what worked. Drop the rest. Do not negotiate.

**Step 5**: Decide if you are a person who does the morning. If yes, the routine is a vote. If no, no routine will save the costume.

The 14 days are not enough to form the habit. They are enough to find the routine. The next 60 days are where the routine becomes the person. Our [2-Minute Rule guide](/journal/two-minute-rule-guide/) shows the protocol that makes the doorway automatic. The morning routine is one version of the doorway. The discipline is the house.
