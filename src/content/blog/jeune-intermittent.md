faq:
  - q: "Does intermittent fasting actually work for weight loss?"
    a: "Yes — but not by magic. The <em>New England Journal of Medicine</em> review most often cited is de Cabo & Mattson 2019 (<a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881139/\">PubMed 31881139</a>); it is careful about what is established, noting that the strongest human findings are weight loss and better cardiometabolic numbers <em>while calories drop</em>, with insulin-sensitivity and inflammation signals promising but not settled. The head-to-head evidence is the useful part: a meta-analysis of randomised trials found intermittent and continuous energy restriction produce broadly similar weight loss and cardiometabolic change (Cioffi et al., <a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30583725/\">J Transl Med 2018</a>). Typical losses in those trials are a few percent of body weight over 8-12 weeks — real, and about the same as counting calories the traditional way."
  - q: "What is the best intermittent fasting schedule for beginners?"
    a: "14:10 (14 hours fasting, 10 eating) for week 1 — essentially just skipping late-night snacking. Progress to 16:8 (skip breakfast or make it late). The 16:8 pattern, eating inside roughly an eight-hour window, is the version most trials have used, and for most people it is also the easiest to keep socially. Neither schedule has a decisive advantage over plain calorie control — pick the one you will still be doing in month two."
  - q: "Is intermittent fasting safe every day?"
    a: "For healthy adults, generally yes — with an honest caveat about how long we know that. Most randomised trials run 8 to 12 weeks, a few up to a year; the comparisons against conventional restriction have not shown extra loss of lean mass or a slowed metabolism when calories and protein are matched (Cioffi et al., <a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30583725/\">J Transl Med 2018</a>), and multi-year human data are still thin. Keep protein up (around 1.6 g/kg) and keep training if you are doing either to protect muscle. Not recommended without medical supervision for: pregnant or breastfeeding women, anyone with a history of eating disorder, type 1 diabetes, or on glucose-lowering medication."

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p2">Intermittent fasting is the most popular eating pattern in the world. But does it actually deliver results? The evidence is promising — and more modest than the headlines.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p3">Intermittent fasting (IF) is not a diet — it is an eating pattern. You cycle between periods of eating and fasting. The most common method is 16:8 — fast for 16 hours, eat for 8.</div>


<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p4"><h2>What the Science Says</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p5"><h3>1. Weight Loss</h3>
The mechanism is unglamorous. Shrink the eating window and many people simply eat fewer calories — and that deficit, not the clock, is what moves the scale. The same window can be used to eat exactly as much in less time. IF works when it makes the deficit easier to live with, and stops working when it does not.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p18">The honest headline from head-to-head trials: when calories are matched, intermittent fasting tends to produce weight loss roughly similar to conventional calorie counting (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30583725/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cioffi et al., J Transl Med 2018</a>). In other words, IF is not metabolically magic — it is a delivery mechanism. For some people a compressed eating window makes the deficit effortless; for others it triggers evening overeating. The right question is not 'does IF work' but 'can I keep this pattern without feeling punished', because adherence, not timing, does the actual work.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p6"><h3>2. Insulin Sensitivity</h3>
Fasting lowers insulin levels and can improve insulin sensitivity in the short term. Whether that translates into lower type 2 diabetes risk over years depends on weight, diet quality and activity; the evidence for fasting specifically is promising but not conclusive.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p7"><h3>3. Cellular Repair</h3>
Fasting upregulates autophagy — a cellular cleanup pathway that recycles damaged proteins. The evidence is strong in animal and cell studies; human data relies on indirect blood markers and remains preliminary. Researchers have not yet confirmed that a 16-hour fast produces the same autophagy magnitude seen in 48–72-hour animal models.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p8"><h3>4. Brain Health</h3>
In animal models, fasting consistently raises BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), a protein that supports neuron growth and cognitive resilience. Human evidence is mixed: some trials report an increase, others show no change or a decrease, and a recent systematic review described the picture as inconsistent. Exercise has far stronger human BDNF data than fasting alone.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p9"><h3>5. Longevity</h3>
Animal studies (yeast, worms, mice) show fasting can extend lifespan, with lower IGF-1 and better stress resistance among the proposed mechanisms. No human lifespan trial exists — the timeline is far too long, and nothing honest will claim otherwise in the meantime. What human trials do show is modest movement in healthspan markers: blood pressure, lipids and inflammatory signals, largely when weight comes off (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881139/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">de Cabo &amp; Mattson, N Engl J Med 2019</a>). Buy the healthspan, not the immortality.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p10"><h2>Common Mistakes</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p11"><h3>1. Eating Too Much During the Eating Window</h3>
IF is not a license to eat junk food. You still need to <a href="/journal/the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady/">eat nutritious meals</a>.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p12"><h3>2. Not Drinking Enough Water</h3>
<a href="/journal/hydratation-performance/">Fasting does not mean dehydrating</a>. Drink plenty of water, black coffee, or tea during your fasting window.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p13"><h3>3. Starting Too Aggressively</h3>
Start with 12:12 (fast 12 hours, eat 12 hours) and gradually increase to 16:8.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p14"><h3>4. Ignoring Your Body</h3>
IF is not for everyone. If you feel dizzy, weak, or unwell, break your fast. Listen to your body.</div>
