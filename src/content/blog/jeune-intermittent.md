faq:
  - q: "Does intermittent fasting actually work for weight loss?"
    a: "Yes — but not by magic. A 2019 New England Journal of Medicine review (de Cabo & Mattson) confirms it works primarily by restricting the eating window (reducing calories naturally) and improving insulin sensitivity. Average result in trials: 3-5 percent body-weight loss over 8-12 weeks — comparable to conventional caloric restriction without hunger counting."
  - q: "What is the best intermittent fasting schedule for beginners?"
    a: "14:10 (14 hours fasting, 10 eating) for week 1 — essentially just skipping late-night snacking. Progress to 16:8 (skip breakfast or make it late). The 16:8 protocol (eating from noon to 8pm) has the most research support and is sustainable socially for most people."
  - q: "Is intermittent fasting safe every day?"
    a: "For healthy adults, generally yes. Daily 16:8 has not produced more lean-mass loss than conventional calorie restriction when protein intake is adequate (around 1.6g/kg) and training continues, and trials up to 12 months show no metabolic slowdown; evidence beyond a year is still thin. Not recommended for: pregnant women, history of eating disorders, type 1 diabetes, or on medication without doctor supervision."

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p2">Intermittent fasting is the most popular eating pattern in the world. But does it actually deliver results? The evidence is promising — and more modest than the headlines.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p3">Intermittent fasting (IF) is not a diet — it is an eating pattern. You cycle between periods of eating and fasting. The most common method is 16:8 — fast for 16 hours, eat for 8.</div>


<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p4"><h2>What the Science Says</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p5"><h3>1. Weight Loss</h3>
IF reduces calorie intake naturally. When you eat in a shorter window, you tend to eat less. This creates a calorie deficit — the only requirement for weight loss.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p18">The honest headline from head-to-head trials: when calories are matched, intermittent fasting tends to produce weight loss roughly similar to conventional calorie counting. In other words, IF is not metabolically magic — it is a delivery mechanism. For some people a compressed eating window makes the deficit effortless; for others it triggers evening overeating. The right question is not 'does IF work' but 'can I keep this pattern without feeling punished', because adherence, not timing, does the actual work.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p6"><h3>2. Insulin Sensitivity</h3>
Fasting lowers insulin levels and can improve insulin sensitivity in the short term. Whether that translates into lower type 2 diabetes risk over years depends on weight, diet quality and activity; the evidence for fasting specifically is promising but not conclusive.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p7"><h3>3. Cellular Repair</h3>
Fasting upregulates autophagy — a cellular cleanup pathway that recycles damaged proteins. The evidence is strong in animal and cell studies; human data relies on indirect blood markers and remains preliminary. Researchers have not yet confirmed that a 16-hour fast produces the same autophagy magnitude seen in 48–72-hour animal models.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p8"><h3>4. Brain Health</h3>
In animal models, fasting consistently raises BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), a protein that supports neuron growth and cognitive resilience. Human evidence is mixed: some trials report an increase, others show no change or a decrease, and a recent systematic review described the picture as inconsistent. Exercise has far stronger human BDNF data than fasting alone.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p9"><h3>5. Longevity</h3>
Animal studies (yeast, worms, mice) show fasting extends lifespan by lowering IGF-1 and improving stress resistance. No human lifespan trial exists — the timeline is too long. Current human evidence points to improved healthspan biomarkers: reduced systemic inflammation, better metabolic flexibility, and lower cardiovascular risk factors.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p10"><h2>Common Mistakes</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p11"><h3>1. Eating Too Much During the Eating Window</h3>
IF is not a license to eat junk food. You still need to <a href="/journal/the-plate-that-keeps-you-steady/">eat nutritious meals</a>.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p12"><h3>2. Not Drinking Enough Water</h3>
<a href="/journal/hydratation-performance/">Fasting does not mean dehydrating</a>. Drink plenty of water, black coffee, or tea during your fasting window.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p13"><h3>3. Starting Too Aggressively</h3>
Start with 12:12 (fast 12 hours, eat 12 hours) and gradually increase to 16:8.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_jeune-intermittent_p14"><h3>4. Ignoring Your Body</h3>
IF is not for everyone. If you feel dizzy, weak, or unwell, break your fast. Listen to your body.</div>
