<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p5">The longer we stay in one place — same streets, same conversations, same problems wearing new outfits — the more competent we feel and the less curious we become. We stop noticing our own city. Worse, we stop noticing ourselves. Days blur into a routine so smooth it never asks us any questions.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p6">I felt this in 2023. I had been living in Casablanca for 3 years. I knew the city. I knew the coffee shops. I knew the routes. I knew which restaurants to avoid. I was comfortable.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p7">And I was bored.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p8">Then I went to Fez for a weekend. I could not read the menu. I did not know which alley was the right one. The simple act of buying bread became a small adventure requiring humility, gesture, patience. Within hours of arriving, I had been demoted from expert back to beginner — and it felt like waking up.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p9"><h2>The Trap of Expertise</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p10">Expertise is a quiet trap. The more you know, the less you learn. The more competent you feel, the less curious you become.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p11">In 2023, I was writing a book about habits and productivity. I felt like an expert. I had read the books. I had tested the systems. I had built my own habits. I knew what worked.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p12">Then I went to Fez, and I could not buy bread without help.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p13">The contrast was shocking. In Casablanca, I was competent. In Fez, I was a beginner. The beginner had to ask for help. The beginner had to gesture. The beginner had to smile and accept the humiliation of not knowing.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p14">And the beginner was more alive than the expert had been in months.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p15"><h2>What Travel Actually Offers</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p16">Travel is not escape. It is recalibration.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p17">The world turns the difficulty back on you. You cannot read the menu. You do not know which line is the right one. The simple act of buying bread becomes a small adventure requiring humility, gesture, patience.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p18">Within hours of arriving, you have been demoted from expert back to beginner — and it feels like waking up.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p19">I learned this in Fez in March 2023. I spent 48 hours there. I got lost three times. I ate bread I did not order. I paid twice for the same thing. I smiled through misunderstandings that were not funny at the time.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p20">But I also felt something I had not felt in months: alive. The beginner had to pay attention. The beginner had to learn. The beginner had to be present.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p21"><h2>The Lessons from the Road</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p22"><h3>Lesson 1: Being Bad at Things Is Survivable</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p23">At home, we avoid embarrassment at all costs. Abroad, embarrassment becomes hourly — mispronounced words, wrong turns, meals ordered by pointing — and each one passes without catastrophe.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p24">In Fez, I ordered "café" and got "thé." I tried to pay with a credit card and the shopkeeper laughed. I asked for directions and was led to the wrong place. Each time, I survived. The world did not end. The embarrassment was temporary. The lesson was permanent.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p25">Being bad at things is survivable. For anyone building anything, that lesson is oxygen.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p26"><h3>Lesson 2: Comfort Is a Metric, Not a Goal</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p27">Comfortable trips confirm who you are. Uncomfortable ones expand it.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p28">In 2023, I went to Fez with a plan. I had a list of places to see. I had a map. I had a guidebook. I was comfortable.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p29">Then I lost the map. Then I lost the guidebook. Then I lost the list of places. I was alone in a medina I did not know, with no plan, no map, no guide.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p30">That is when the trip started.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p31">The discomfort was the point. The uncertainty was the teacher. The beginner mind was the goal.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p32"><h3>Lesson 3: The World Is Bigger Than Your Problems</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p33">You come back to your own street and see it briefly as a stranger would — smaller in some ways, precious in others. Problems that looked permanent reveal themselves as local. Habits that felt inevitable reveal themselves as optional.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p34">In Fez, I saw people living with less than I had, and they were happier. I saw people working harder for less, and they were more grateful. I saw a world that did not care about my problems — and somehow, that made my problems smaller.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p35">Somewhere out there, millions of people live happily without doing things the way you do them. That knowledge loosens something. It makes room.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p36"><h3>Lesson 4: Keep One Habit Alive on the Road</h3></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p37">Travel changes your view. It should not change your discipline.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p38">In 2023, I kept two habits alive on the road: morning pages and push-ups. Every morning, I wrote three pages by hand. Every morning, I did 20 push-ups. The habits were small. They were portable. They told my identity that travel had changed my view, not my discipline.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p39">The beginner mind needs an anchor. The anchor is the habit that survives the trip.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p40"><h2>A Few Truths Worth Carrying Onto Any Plane</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p41">- <strong>Go where you know no one.</strong> Comfortable trips confirm who you are. Uncomfortable ones expand it.
- <strong>Plan less than feels safe.</strong> The gap in the itinerary is where the story will come from.
- <strong>Talk to strangers deliberately.</strong> Every place is ordinary to someone. Their ordinariness is your education.
- <strong>Keep one habit alive on the road.</strong> It tells your identity that travel changed your view, not your discipline.
- <strong>Bring the lesson home.</strong> The trip ends. The recalibrated eyes should not.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p42"><h2>The Deeper Return</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p43">There is the deeper return, the one that shows up weeks later. You come back to your own street and see it briefly as a stranger would — smaller in some ways, precious in others. Problems that looked permanent reveal themselves as local. Habits that felt inevitable reveal themselves as optional.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p44">In 2023, I came back from Fez and saw Casablanca differently. The coffee shop I thought was essential was just a coffee shop. The route I thought was the only way was just one of many. The problems I thought were permanent were just habits.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p45">Travel had recalibrated my eyes. The recalibration lasted for weeks. Maybe months.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p46">You do not need a long trip or an expensive one. A bus to a town whose name you cannot pronounce will do. The requirement was never distance. It is unfamiliarity. Go where your competence means nothing, where every hour asks you to learn.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_beginner-again_p47">Beginner again. It is the hungriest you will feel all year.</div>
