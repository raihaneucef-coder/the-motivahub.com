# Go Where You Are a Beginner Again

Expertise is a quiet trap.

The longer we stay in one place — same streets, same conversations, same problems wearing new outfits — the more competent we feel and the less curious we become. We stop noticing our own city. Worse, we stop noticing ourselves. Days blur into a routine so smooth it never asks us any questions.

I felt this in 2023. I had been living in Casablanca for 3 years. I knew the city. I knew the coffee shops. I knew the routes. I knew which restaurants to avoid. I was comfortable.

And I was bored.

Then I went to Fez for a weekend. I could not read the menu. I did not know which alley was the right one. The simple act of buying bread became a small adventure requiring humility, gesture, patience. Within hours of arriving, I had been demoted from expert back to beginner — and it felt like waking up.

## The Trap of Expertise

Expertise is a quiet trap. The more you know, the less you learn. The more competent you feel, the less curious you become.

In 2023, I was writing a book about habits and productivity. I felt like an expert. I had read the books. I had tested the systems. I had built my own habits. I knew what worked.

Then I went to Fez, and I could not buy bread without help.

The contrast was shocking. In Casablanca, I was competent. In Fez, I was a beginner. The beginner had to ask for help. The beginner had to gesture. The beginner had to smile and accept the humiliation of not knowing.

And the beginner was more alive than the expert had been in months.

## What Travel Actually Offers

Travel is not escape. It is recalibration.

The world turns the difficulty back on you. You cannot read the menu. You do not know which line is the right one. The simple act of buying bread becomes a small adventure requiring humility, gesture, patience.

Within hours of arriving, you have been demoted from expert back to beginner — and it feels like waking up.

I learned this in Fez in March 2023. I spent 48 hours there. I got lost three times. I ate bread I did not order. I paid twice for the same thing. I smiled through misunderstandings that were not funny at the time.

But I also felt something I had not felt in months: alive. The beginner had to pay attention. The beginner had to learn. The beginner had to be present.

## The Lessons from the Road

### Lesson 1: Being Bad at Things Is Survivable

At home, we avoid embarrassment at all costs. Abroad, embarrassment becomes hourly — mispronounced words, wrong turns, meals ordered by pointing — and each one passes without catastrophe.

In Fez, I ordered "café" and got "thé." I tried to pay with a credit card and the shopkeeper laughed. I asked for directions and was led to the wrong place. Each time, I survived. The world did not end. The embarrassment was temporary. The lesson was permanent.

Being bad at things is survivable. For anyone building anything, that lesson is oxygen.

### Lesson 2: Comfort Is a Metric, Not a Goal

Comfortable trips confirm who you are. Uncomfortable ones expand it.

In 2023, I went to Fez with a plan. I had a list of places to see. I had a map. I had a guidebook. I was comfortable.

Then I lost the map. Then I lost the guidebook. Then I lost the list of places. I was alone in a medina I did not know, with no plan, no map, no guide.

That is when the trip started.

The discomfort was the point. The uncertainty was the teacher. The beginner mind was the goal.

### Lesson 3: The World Is Bigger Than Your Problems

You come back to your own street and see it briefly as a stranger would — smaller in some ways, precious in others. Problems that looked permanent reveal themselves as local. Habits that felt inevitable reveal themselves as optional.

In Fez, I saw people living with less than I had, and they were happier. I saw people working harder for less, and they were more grateful. I saw a world that did not care about my problems — and somehow, that made my problems smaller.

Somewhere out there, millions of people live happily without doing things the way you do them. That knowledge loosens something. It makes room.

### Lesson 4: Keep One Habit Alive on the Road

Travel changes your view. It should not change your discipline.

In 2023, I kept two habits alive on the road: morning pages and push-ups. Every morning, I wrote three pages by hand. Every morning, I did 20 push-ups. The habits were small. They were portable. They told my identity that travel had changed my view, not my discipline.

The beginner mind needs an anchor. The anchor is the habit that survives the trip.

## A Few Truths Worth Carrying Onto Any Plane

- **Go where you know no one.** Comfortable trips confirm who you are. Uncomfortable ones expand it.
- **Plan less than feels safe.** The gap in the itinerary is where the story will come from.
- **Talk to strangers deliberately.** Every place is ordinary to someone. Their ordinariness is your education.
- **Keep one habit alive on the road.** It tells your identity that travel changed your view, not your discipline.
- **Bring the lesson home.** The trip ends. The recalibrated eyes should not.

## The Deeper Return

There is the deeper return, the one that shows up weeks later. You come back to your own street and see it briefly as a stranger would — smaller in some ways, precious in others. Problems that looked permanent reveal themselves as local. Habits that felt inevitable reveal themselves as optional.

In 2023, I came back from Fez and saw Casablanca differently. The coffee shop I thought was essential was just a coffee shop. The route I thought was the only way was just one of many. The problems I thought were permanent were just habits.

Travel had recalibrated my eyes. The recalibration lasted for weeks. Maybe months.

You do not need a long trip or an expensive one. A bus to a town whose name you cannot pronounce will do. The requirement was never distance. It is unfamiliarity. Go where your competence means nothing, where every hour asks you to learn.

Beginner again. It is the hungriest you will feel all year.
