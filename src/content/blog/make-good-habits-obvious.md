<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p2">You do not need more willpower — you need a clearer path. Design your world so good habits are easy to see and bad ones are hard to find.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p3">Willpower is not the problem. Visibility is.</div>


<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p4">We keep fruit in the back of the fridge and chips on the counter, then wonder why we eat chips. We keep the book in the drawer and the phone on the pillow, then wonder why we scroll.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p5"><strong>You do not need more discipline. You need a clearer path.</strong></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p12"><h2>The Cue Comes Before the Motivation</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p13">Every habit already runs on the same sequence: a cue signals that a reward is close, the cue makes you want it, the wanting drives an action, and the action closes the loop. That is the order James Clear describes in the Four Laws of Behavior Change, and the order is the point. Motivation rarely arrives first and then produces action. The cue arrives first, and what you do next tends to follow whichever cue is easiest to notice. If the book is in a drawer and the phone is on the pillow, the pillow is the one you act on.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p14">So the useful first question is not 'how badly do I want this?' It is 'what is standing in front of me at the hour this habit is supposed to happen?'</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p15"><h2>Do a Cue Audit of the Rooms You Actually Pass Through</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p16">You cannot redesign cues you have not noticed. Walk the places where your habits actually happen, at the hour they happen, and write down what you see first in each one: the desk in the morning, the kitchen mid-afternoon, the bedroom at night. Whatever sits in the line of sight on the way to what you already do without thinking is the current cue for that behaviour.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p17">- <strong>What is visible that feeds a habit you want to drop?</strong> The phone on the nightstand, the packaging on the counter, the remote on the arm of the sofa.
- <strong>What is hidden that feeds a habit you want to keep?</strong> The book in the drawer, the shoes in the closet, the fruit behind the yogurt in the fridge.
- <strong>What would swapping them cost?</strong> Usually a minute, and nothing else.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p18">That short inventory is the whole first step, and it separates an environment you inherited from convenience from one you chose. The longer piece on <a href="/journal/environnement-beat-volonte/">why environment beats willpower</a> works through the same mechanism in more detail.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p6">Make good habits obvious and bad habits invisible:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p7">- <strong>Want to read?</strong> <a href="/journal/how-to-read-30-books-a-year/">Put the book on your pillow</a>.
- <strong>Want to move?</strong> Put your shoes by the door.
- <strong>Want to scroll less?</strong> Put your phone in another room, not in your hand.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p8">And the reverse is just as powerful:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p9">- <strong>Want to snack less?</strong> Keep the snacks on the highest shelf, not the counter.
- <strong>Want to watch less?</strong> Unplug the TV or log out. One extra step is enough to make you choose better.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p19"><h2>Make Bad Habits Invisible with Friction, Not Prohibition</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p20">Removing a cue is not the same as banning a behaviour, and the difference is where most attempts fail. Prohibition leaves the reward sitting there and asks you to refuse it again and again: every snack in the bowl is another time to say no, and saying no is tiring work. Friction changes the shape of the choice instead. The snack that is not in the house is never offered. The phone in the drawer does not light up every few minutes. The app that has left the home screen is a search away rather than a tap away. Each of those edits keeps the option available for when you genuinely want it and removes the prompt that made it the default. You are not resisting temptation; you are being handed it less often.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p21"><h2>Why 'Obvious' Stops Being Obvious</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p22">Cues fade. A prompt you walk past every day stops being read and starts being scenery; the shoes by the door become part of the door. That is why a setup that worked in the first week can go quiet by the sixth, and why the fix is maintenance rather than more discipline: move the book to a different pillow, put the glass where the mug usually sits, change the home screen once a month, or re-attach the habit to something you already do without thinking, which is what <a href="/journal/habit-stacking-made-simple/">habit stacking</a> is for. Rotation is not proof that the original design failed. It is how a design keeps working.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p10"><a href="/journal/regle-deux-minutes/">Design beats discipline</a> every time. You do not have to be the person with the strongest will. You just have to be the person who made the good choice the easy choice.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_make-good-habits-obvious_p11">Make it obvious today. Your future self will thank you for the clear path.</div>
