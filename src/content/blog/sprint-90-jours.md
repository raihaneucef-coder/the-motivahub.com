faq:
  - q: "What is a 90-day growth sprint?"
    a: "A fixed 90-day period in which you push one or two specific areas of your growth — a skill, your health, your writing, your income — with a defined endpoint. It has three parts: a goal you can describe in one sentence, weekly milestones that show you are moving, and daily actions you can actually perform. At day 90 you stop, review the evidence and decide what the next sprint carries."
  - q: "Why 90 days instead of a year or a month?"
    a: "A year is too far away to create urgency, so 'later' costs nothing. A month is too short to produce a result you can measure. Ninety days is the shortest window that still allows a real change, and the longest one that still feels like a deadline. It also matches the quarterly rhythm most work already runs on, so reviews and planning line up."
  - q: "How many goals should a 90-day sprint hold?"
    a: "Two, and one is better. In 13 weeks you can genuinely move one or two things. Three or four sounds efficient and turns into four partial efforts. The scarcity is the point: choosing what is out of scope is what makes the sprint produce something you can measure."
  - q: "What happens after the 90 days are over?"
    a: "You run a review, then a handoff. Either the result becomes a standing habit that needs no sprint, or it gets a new sprint with a higher bar, or you drop it because the review showed it was not actually yours. What you should not do is start the next sprint the same evening without writing anything down."

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p4">The 90-day sprint is the most effective goal-setting timeframe. It is long enough to accomplish something meaningful — and short enough to create urgency.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p19"><h2>What Is a 90-Day Growth Sprint?</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p20">A 90-day growth sprint is a fixed 90-day window pointed at one or two areas of your growth, with a written start date, a written end date, and a defined result. Not a vague intention — a result you could prove to a stranger.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p21">Three parts make it a sprint rather than a resolution:
<ul><li><strong>A goal stated in one sentence.</strong> "Run a 10K in under 60 minutes by 14 March."</li><li><strong>Weekly milestones.</strong> The checkpoints that tell you, every Sunday, whether you are on track.</li><li><strong>Daily actions.</strong> The small repeatable work that produces the milestones.</li></ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p22">The word "growth" is what separates it from a work deadline. A deadline asks you to deliver something for someone else. A growth sprint asks you to change something about what you can do — and it is the same 90 days either way, which is why the structure matters more than the motivation.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p5"><h2>Why 90 Days Works</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p6"><h3>1. Urgency</h3>
90 days is close enough to feel real. You cannot afford to procrastinate when the deadline is three months away, not twelve.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p7"><h3>2. Focus</h3>
With only 90 days, you cannot pursue 10 goals. You must choose 1-2 that matter most. This forced focus is a gift.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p8"><h3>3. Momentum</h3>
90 days is long enough to build momentum, but short enough to see results before motivation fades.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p9"><h3>4. Adaptability</h3>
The world changes fast. A 90-day goal allows you to adjust every quarter based on what you have learned.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p27"><h3>5. A deadline you can feel</h3>
Twelve-month plans fail on a well-documented habit of the mind: the further away a deadline is, the cheaper "later" feels. Ninety days removes that discount. You are on week 4 before comfort can set in.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p28"><h2>The Math of a Sprint</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p29">90 days is 13 weeks. Six focused sessions a week gives you 78. That number turns an intimidating goal into a budget:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p30"><ul><li><strong>Writing 30 articles:</strong> one finished article every two and a half sessions.</li><li><strong>Saving €3,000:</strong> €231 a month, or €54 a week — a number you can check on a Sunday.</li><li><strong>Running a 10K from the couch:</strong> 13 weeks of walk-and-run progressions, one long session per week.</li><li><strong>Learning a tool for work:</strong> 78 short sessions, which is enough to become dangerous in it.</li></ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p31">If the goal cannot be expressed in sessions, it is not a sprint goal yet. Either shrink the goal or extend the window — and if you extend the window past 90 days, you have written a yearly resolution again.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p10"><h2>How to Run a 90-Day Sprint</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p11"><h3>Day 1: Write it down before you start</h3>
One page, four lines: the goal, the day it ends, the two or three weekly milestones, the daily action. A goal that lives only in your head cannot be reviewed, and a goal that cannot be reviewed cannot be hit.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p12"><h3>Day 2 to day 44: Execute</h3>
Do the daily action. Review the week every Sunday in five minutes, and adjust the plan when needed — not the goal. The mid-sprint check at day 45 is where most people discover they have been drifting since week three.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p34"><h3>Day 45: The reset</h3>
Ask one question: "If I started this sprint today, knowing what I know now, would I keep this goal?" If the answer is no, cut it here and spend the last six weeks on something you actually want. Quitting at day 45 is a decision. Quitting at day 70 in silence is a failure.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p13"><h3>Day 90: Review</h3>
Five written questions:
<ol><li>Did the milestones land, and when did they stop landing?</li><li>What was the actual daily action rate, not the intended one?</li><li>What made it harder than expected?</li><li>What did the result teach me about what I want?</li><li>Does this become a habit, a new sprint, or a closed file?</li></ol></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p14"><h2>Real-World 90-Day Goals</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p15">A good sprint goal has a number and a date inside it. Compare:
<ul><li><strong>Fitness:</strong> "Run a 10K in 90 days" — not "get fit".</li><li><strong>Finance:</strong> "Save €3,000 in 90 days" — not "spend less".</li><li><strong>Career:</strong> "Complete the certification by day 90" — not "progress at work".</li><li><strong>Learning:</strong> "Finish the course and build one small project with it" — not "learn more".</li><li><strong>Writing:</strong> "Write 30 articles in 90 days" — not "write more".</li></ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p37"><h2>The Four Ways a Sprint Fails</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p38"><ol><li><strong>Too many goals.</strong> Four goals in 13 weeks is four partial results. Pick two.</li><li><strong>No weekly review.</strong> Without a Sunday check, drift is invisible until day 80.</li><li><strong>Changing the goal on a bad day.</strong> The urge to rewrite the goal almost always arrives after a weak week. That is the goal protecting itself. Change the plan, not the goal — and only at the day-45 reset.</li><li><strong>Treating day 90 as an ending.</strong> The result disappears in a month if nothing carries it. Decide at the review what stays: a habit, a new sprint, or a closed file.</li></ol></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p39"><h2>What Comes After the Sprint</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p40">Three outcomes, and all three are fine:
<ul><li><strong>It becomes a standard.</strong> The 10K is run; now you keep running without a deadline.</li><li><strong>It becomes a bigger sprint.</strong> The same goal with a higher bar, starting Monday, written on the back of the review.</li><li><strong>It closes.</strong> Some goals were borrowed from someone else's list. Dropping one at day 90, on purpose, is a result.</li></ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_sprint-90-jours_p41">Four sprints make a year. That is enough time to change something real about your body, your craft and your income — without ever having to hold a resolution in your head for twelve months.</div>
