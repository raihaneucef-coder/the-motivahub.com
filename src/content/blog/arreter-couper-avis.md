**Caring what people think is human. Letting it control your life is a choice.** Most people are focused on themselves, not you. The spotlight effect is real: you think people notice you more than they do. The goal is not to stop caring entirely — it is to care less about strangers' opinions and more about the opinions of people who know and respect you.

# How to Stop Caring What People Think of You

The average person spends 5 hours a day worrying about what others think. That is 75 days per year spent on opinions that do not matter.

I know this because I was that person.

In 2023, I spent 3 weeks rewriting an article because I was afraid a specific person would judge it. I did not send it to that person. I did not even know if that person would read it. But the fear was enough to make me rewrite it 7 times. The original version was better. The 7th version was safe.

That is the cost of caring too much about other people's opinions: you optimize for safety instead of quality.

## Why We Care What People Think

### 1. Evolutionary Survival

For thousands of years, social rejection meant death. Your brain is wired to care about acceptance — even when it is no longer dangerous.

In a tribe of 150 people, being expelled meant starvation. Your brain remembers this. It still treats social disapproval as a survival threat, even when the stakes are a social media comment or a colleague's opinion.

### 2. Need for Belonging

Humans are social animals. We need to belong. But there is a difference between belonging and approval-seeking.

Belonging is finding your tribe. Approval-seeking is trying to please everyone. The first is healthy. The second is impossible.

### 3. Comparison Trap

When you compare yourself to others, you naturally care about their opinions. But comparison is a losing game — there will always be someone ahead.

In 2023, I compared my writing to 5 other writers I followed. I thought they were better. I thought their audience was larger. I thought their work was more polished. I was wrong on all three counts. But the comparison made me afraid to publish.

## The Real Cost of Caring Too Much

The cost is not just time. It is identity.

When you care too much about what people think, you start writing for an imaginary audience. You start making decisions based on what will impress others instead of what is true for you.

In my case, I spent 3 weeks rewriting an article for an audience of one person who might never read it. The original version was honest. The 7th version was safe. The safe version got fewer shares, fewer comments, and fewer genuine connections.

The honest version would have done better. But I never published it.

## How to Stop Caring

### 1. Realize the Spotlight Effect

You think people notice you more than they do. In reality, everyone is focused on themselves. The spotlight effect is an illusion.

I tested this in 2023. I wore a shirt with a small stain to a coffee shop. I was convinced everyone was looking at it. I asked my wife later. She had not noticed. The person across from me had not noticed. The barista had not noticed.

Your brain is not a reliable narrator of how much people are watching you.

### 2. Ask: "Will This Matter in 5 Years?"

If the answer is no, do not spend more than 5 minutes worrying about it.

I use this test before every article I publish. If the fear is "someone might disagree with me," I ask: will this matter in 5 years? The answer is almost always no. The article will be forgotten. The disagreement will be forgotten. The only thing that matters is whether the article is true and useful.

### 3. Accept Disapproval

Not everyone will like you. And that is fine. Trying to please everyone is the fastest way to please no one — including yourself.

In 2024, I published an article that was critical of a popular productivity method. I received 3 emails from people who disagreed with me. I also received 12 emails from people who said the article helped them see the method differently. The 3 disapproving voices were loud. The 12 approving voices were quiet. But the 12 were the ones who mattered.

### 4. Define Your Values

When you know what you stand for, the opinions of others lose their power. You cannot be swayed by opinions if you are anchored by values.

My values are: honesty, usefulness, long-term thinking. When I make a decision, I ask: does this align with my values? If yes, the opinions of others matter less. If no, the opinions of others matter more, because they are signaling that I am off course.

### 5. Take Action Despite Fear

Confidence is not the absence of fear. It is acting despite the fear. Every time you act despite caring what people think, the fear loses its grip.

In 2023, I published an article about my biggest professional failure. I was afraid people would think less of me. I published it anyway. The article got more shares than anything I had written that year. Three people emailed me to say it helped them stop hiding their own failures.

Fear is not a stop sign. It is a signal that you are doing something that matters.

> *You would not worry so much about what others think of you if you realized how seldom they do. — Eleanor Roosevelt*


## The Practice: 30 Days of Not Caring

If you want to build this skill, here is the protocol:

**Week 1: Notice.** Every time you catch yourself worrying about what someone thinks, write it down. "I am worried that X will think Y." Do not fix it. Just notice.

**Week 2: Ask the 5-year test.** For each worry, ask: will this matter in 5 years? If no, downgrade the worry. If yes, investigate why.

**Week 3: Do one thing daily that you would normally avoid.** Wear the shirt with the stain. Share the unpopular opinion. Post the imperfect draft. The goal is not to be reckless. The goal is to prove to yourself that the world does not end when you stop performing.

**Week 4: Reflect.** At the end of the month, look at your list of worries. How many of them actually happened? How many of them mattered? How many of them were about things you cannot control?

Most people find that 80% of their worries never happened, and the 20% that did happen were not as bad as they expected.


## What Comes After 30 Days

Once you have practiced not caring for 30 days, you can extend it:

- **Month 2:** Add a weekly "no approval" challenge. Do one thing per week that you would normally do for approval.
- **Month 3:** Add a monthly "values check." Review your decisions from the month. How many were based on your values? How many were based on fear of judgment?
- **Month 4:** Build a "no opinion" muscle. When someone gives you unsolicited feedback, thank them and move on. You do not have to agree. You do not have to explain.

Each layer compounds. Each one is small. Each one survives bad days.

This is the system I am still building. The 30-day practice was the foundation. Everything else is built on top.
