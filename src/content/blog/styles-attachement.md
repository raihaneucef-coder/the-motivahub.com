<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p2">Your attachment style shapes how you love, how you fight, and how you handle separation. Understanding it changes everything.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p3">Have you ever wondered why some people pull away when things get close, while others cling desperately? The answer lies in your attachment style — a pattern of relating to others that forms in childhood.</div>


<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p4"><h2>The 4 Attachment Styles</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p5"><h3>1. Secure</h3>
You are comfortable with intimacy and independence. You trust easily, communicate openly, and <a href="/journal/secure-self/">do not fear abandonment</a>. In large US community samples, close to six in ten adults screen as secure — the exact share shifts with the survey instrument, but secure remains the modal style.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p6"><h3>2. Anxious-Preoccupied</h3>
You crave intimacy but fear abandonment. You need constant reassurance, worry about your partner's feelings, and become clingy under stress. Anxious-preoccupied shows up in roughly 15-25% of adults depending on the sample.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p7"><h3>3. Dismissive-Avoidant</h3>
You value independence over intimacy. You suppress your feelings, pull away when things get close, and struggle with vulnerability. Dismissive-avoidant is measured at around 20-25% in most Western samples.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p8"><h3>4. Fearful-Avoidant</h3>
You want intimacy but fear it. You push people away when they get close, then pull them back when they leave. This is the least common of the four and the one most associated with unresolved trauma or inconsistent early caregiving.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p16">Two honest caveats keep this useful. First, styles are distributions, not boxes: most people are a mix and score differently across relationships and over time, so treat the four types as a map of tendencies, not a diagnosis. Second, 'can you change' has an affirmative research answer — Mikulincer's work on 'secure-base priming' and studies of earned security show that consistent relationships and therapy do shift attachment behavior. The style explains your default, not your ceiling.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p9"><h2>Can You Change Your Attachment Style?</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p10">Yes. Attachment styles are not fixed. With awareness, therapy, and practice, you can develop a secure attachment style. The first step is recognizing your current style.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p11"><h2>How to Build Secure Attachment</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_styles-attachement_p12">1. Learn your style — understand your patterns
2. <a href="/journal/love-is-a-practice/">Communicate your needs</a> — do not expect others to guess
3. Practice vulnerability — share your feelings even when it is scary
4. Build trust through consistency — show up reliably
5. Seek therapy if needed — a professional can help you rewrite your patterns</div>
