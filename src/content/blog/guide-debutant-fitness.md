faq:
  - q: "How do I start going to the gym as a complete beginner?"
    a: "Start smaller than feels useful. Go to the gym twice a week for fifteen minutes and do one simple machine or bodyweight exercise each visit. The first goal is not fitness — it is learning the building, the equipment and the routine well enough that going stops feeling intimidating. Add time and weight only once showing up is automatic."
  - q: "How many days a week should a beginner train?"
    a: "Two to three non-consecutive days is enough to build the habit and still recover. Training more than that in the first weeks usually produces soreness and burnout rather than faster results. Consistency across months matters far more than intensity inside a single week."
  - q: "How long before working out becomes a habit?"
    a: "There is no fixed number, whatever the posters in the gym say. The classic University College London study (<a href=\"https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674\">Lally et al., 2010</a>) found a median of about 66 days for a simple daily behaviour to start feeling automatic, with a range from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the habit. The variable you control is not intensity but predictability — the same time, the same place, attached to something you already do every day."
  - q: "Do I need a workout plan or a trainer to start?"
    a: "No. A plan you actually follow beats an optimal plan you abandon. A fixed beginner routine of five or six exercises — one push, one pull, one leg movement and a short walk — is enough for your first few months. Add coaching later, once the habit itself is secure."

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p2">Starting a fitness routine is easy. Sticking to one is hard. Here is the beginner-friendly guide to building a fitness habit that lasts. More in <a href="/topics/sport/">Sport</a>.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p3">You do not need a perfect workout plan. You do not need expensive equipment. You do not need a personal trainer. You need one thing: the ability to show up consistently. Everything else can be learned.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p4"><h2>Step 1: Start Embarrassingly Small</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p5">The biggest mistake beginners make is starting too big. They go from zero to 5 days a week, 1 hour per session. By week 2, they are burned out and back on the couch.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p6">Start with 10 minutes. That is it. 10 minutes of walking, stretching, or bodyweight exercises. The goal is not to get fit — the goal is to build the habit of showing up.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p7"><h2>Step 2: Attach to an Existing Habit</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p8">Use the formula: "After [existing habit], I will [new habit]." This is the same habit stacking method explained in <a href="/journal/habit-stacking-routine/">our complete guide</a>.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p9">Examples:
<ul>
<li>"After I pour my coffee, I will do 10 push-ups"</li>
<li>"After I brush my teeth, I will stretch for 5 minutes"</li>
<li>"After I eat lunch, I will walk for 10 minutes"</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p10"><h2>Step 3: Track Your Progress</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p11">Use our <a href="/tracker/">Habit Tracker</a> to mark each day you exercise. Seeing your streak grow is motivating. Breaking a streak is painful. Both drive consistency.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p12"><h2>Step 4: Gradually Increase</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p13">After 2 weeks of 10 minutes, increase to 15. After another 2 weeks, increase to 20. Keep going until you reach 30-45 minutes. This gradual approach prevents burnout and builds confidence. Expect a little muscle soreness along the way, and learn to tell it apart from pain: <a href="/journal/body-votes-first/">body votes first</a> explains how to read the difference before an injury reads it for you.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p21">A number that protects you from impatience: <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Phillippa Lally's now-classic University College London study</a> tracked habits forming over a range of 18 to 254 days, with a median around 66 — not the 21 days pop culture repeats. So when week three feels pointless, it is not a sign to quit; automaticity is simply still under construction. The 'start embarrassingly small, then gradually increase' plan is built for exactly that slow, uneven curve.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p14"><h2>Step 5: Celebrate Small Wins</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p15">Did you exercise 3 days in a row? Celebrate. Did you increase your reps? Celebrate. Small wins create momentum. Momentum creates habits. Habits create transformation.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_h2f"><h2>Missed a Week? Restart Without Starting Over</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p22">A week away happens: work, illness, family, or simply a routine that fell apart. The mistake is treating the gap as a reset. You do not need to begin at zero, and you do not need to "catch up" by doubling sessions. The habit is a behaviour you return to, not a streak that has to be perfect.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p23">Some fitness does fade. A review of short-term detraining — measured in weeks rather than months — finds partial losses in cardiorespiratory fitness and endurance performance, while noting that the changes are more moderate in recently trained people (<a href="https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200030020-00002" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Mujika &amp; Padilla, 2000</a>). A meta-analysis of resistance-training cessation found that strength and power decline after a layoff, with the size of the loss increasing with the duration of the break (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12047" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Bosquet et al., 2013</a>). That is a reason to resume soon, not a reason to panic.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p24">Progress is also more forgiving than the streak view suggests. In a small trial, men who trained in cycles with planned breaks between blocks gained muscle size and strength similar to men who trained continuously (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-012-2511-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ogasawara et al., 2013</a>). The study is small and is not a licence to plan long breaks, but it makes the practical point: an interruption is not an erasure.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_guide-debutant-fitness_p25">How to restart: go back to the last session that felt comfortable, or one step below it. Use the same time and cue that worked before. Make the first session deliberately easy; the goal is to show up, not to repay the missed week. Then continue the progression from Step 4. If the absence was caused by pain or illness, get medical advice before loading the area again. The habit survives on returns, not on perfect attendance.</div>
