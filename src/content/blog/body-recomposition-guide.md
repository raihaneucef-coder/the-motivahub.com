<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p1">You can lose fat and build muscle at the same time. Not the way the before-and-after photos sell it, and not at the same speed for everyone — but it is real, and it is one of the most useful things to understand about your body.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p2">Most advice forces you to pick a lane: cut, or bulk. Lose weight, or gain weight. That framing is not wrong, it is just incomplete. Your body is not a single number on a scale. It is a mix of fat and muscle, and you can move those two in opposite directions if you set the right conditions.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p3">Here is the honest version: what "recomposition" actually means, the exact numbers for each goal, and the handful of habits that decide whether any of it works. No magic, no forbidden foods, no eight-week shred.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p4"><h2>Start here: maintenance, deficit, surplus</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p5">Everything about changing your body runs through one idea — energy balance. Your body burns a certain number of calories a day just to stay you: breathing, circulating blood, thinking, moving. Add up your resting burn plus your activity and you get your maintenance level. Eat at it and your weight holds. That number is the starting point for every plan.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p6"><ul>
<li><strong>Maintenance</strong> — eat what you burn. Weight stays flat. This is your reference point, and you can find it by eating normally for two weeks and noting the calories where your weight holds steady.</li>
<li><strong>Deficit</strong> — eat below maintenance. Your body taps stored energy. This is how you lose fat.</li>
<li><strong>Surplus</strong> — eat above maintenance. Your body has extra to build with. This is how you gain weight, ideally mostly muscle.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p7">Calories decide the direction of the scale. What you do in the kitchen and the gym decides what that change is made of. Two people can both lose four kilos — one loses mostly fat, the other loses fat and hard-won muscle. The number is identical; the body is not. That is why the next variable matters more than any diet trick:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p8"><ul>
<li><strong>Protein first.</strong> Around 1.6 g per kg of body weight per day maximises muscle gain from training (Morton et al., 2018). Spread it over three to four meals.</li>
<li><strong>Fat for hormones.</strong> Roughly 0.6 to 1 g per kg from olive oil, nuts, avocado, egg yolks — enough to keep you and your hormones healthy.</li>
<li><strong>Carbs fill the rest.</strong> Rice, potatoes, oats, fruit, bread. Put most of them around your training, when your body uses them best.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p9"><h2>Case 1: lose fat without losing muscle</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p10">If your goal is to get leaner, the mistake is speed. A big deficit burns fat fast, but it also burns muscle and torches your willpower. The research on natural athletes is consistent: aim to lose about <strong>0.5 to 1% of your body weight per week</strong> (Helms et al., 2014). Slower than that and you stall; faster and an increasing share of what you lose is lean tissue.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p11">The four levers that keep the weight you lose as fat as possible:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p12"><ul>
<li><strong>Keep protein high.</strong> In a deficit, push toward 2.2 to 3.0 g per kg (Ruiz-Castellano et al., 2021). Protein protects muscle and is the most filling macro, which makes the whole diet easier to stick to.</li>
<li><strong>Keep lifting.</strong> Resistance training is the signal that tells your body to hold onto muscle while it releases fat. Drop it and a large part of the loss is muscle.</li>
<li><strong>Use a small deficit.</strong> Cut a few hundred calories, not a thousand. Let the weeks do the work.</li>
<li><strong>Walk more, not harder.</strong> Extra daily steps raise your burn without wrecking your recovery the way a pile of hard cardio does.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p13"><h2>Case 2: gain muscle without dumping fat</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p14">Gaining weight is easy. Gaining mostly muscle is not. A big "dirty bulk" surplus adds size fast, but a lot of it is fat you will spend months removing. The calmer approach: eat a <strong>small surplus</strong> — roughly 10 to 20% above maintenance — and let the scale climb slowly, about 0.25 to 0.5% of your body weight per week. Keep protein at that same 1.6 g per kg, and let progressive overload in the gym be the reason the extra energy becomes muscle instead of fat.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p15">If the scale is not moving up over two or three weeks, add a small amount of food — a scoop of oats, an extra serving of rice, a handful of nuts. If your waist is growing faster than your lifts, ease the surplus back. You are steering toward muscle, not toward the biggest number you can eat.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p16"><h2>Can you really do both at once?</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p17">Yes — and the reason is simple once you see it. Building muscle costs energy, and stored body fat is energy. If you carry extra fat, your body can pull from that reserve to power new muscle while the scale barely moves. A 2017 review in the <em>Strength and Conditioning Journal</em> concluded that body recomposition is possible even in trained people, not just beginners (Barakat et al., 2017).</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p18">It works best for three groups:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p19"><ul>
<li><strong>New lifters.</strong> "Beginner gains" are real — an untrained muscle responds to almost any stimulus, so you can build even in a slight deficit.</li>
<li><strong>People with higher body fat.</strong> More stored energy means your body needs less food energy to build with.</li>
<li><strong>Those returning after a break.</strong> Muscle memory rebuilds lost size quickly.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p20">If you are already lean and have trained for years, you can still recompose, but slowly — and a traditional cut-then-bulk sequence will likely get you there faster. This guide is written for the majority who are not yet in that position.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p21"><h2>The training rule that drives everything: progressive overload</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p22">Whether you are in a deficit, a surplus, or holding at maintenance, one thing decides how much of your effort turns into muscle: you have to keep asking your muscles to do a little more over time. Add a rep, add a little weight, or improve your technique on the same lift. That is progressive overload, and it is the signal your body cannot ignore.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p23">The practical version, backed by the hypertrophy research (Schoenfeld et al., 2017):</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p24"><ul>
<li><strong>Train each muscle about twice a week.</strong> Three to four sessions total. Full-body or upper/lower beats a once-a-week "bro split" for most people.</li>
<li><strong>Build around compound lifts.</strong> Squat, hinge, press, row, carry. They give the most signal per set.</li>
<li><strong>Take most sets close to failure.</strong> Finish with about one to three reps still in the tank.</li>
<li><strong>Start around 10 hard sets per muscle per week</strong> and add slowly.</li>
<li><strong>Write it down.</strong> A number that climbs week after week is the clearest proof you are building muscle — clearer than the scale.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p25"><h2>What to eat before and after training</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p26">Forget the panic about the "anabolic window." The evidence is clear that your <strong>total protein for the day matters far more than the exact minute you eat it</strong> (Aragon & Schoenfeld, 2013). The window is hours wide, not thirty minutes. That said, a little structure helps you perform and recover:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p27"><ul>
<li><strong>Before (one to three hours out):</strong> a meal with protein and some carbs — the carbs fuel the session, the protein primes repair. Training fasted is fine for most people if you hit protein later.</li>
<li><strong>After (within a few hours):</strong> a good protein serving, roughly 0.4 g per kg, alongside carbs to refill what you burned.</li>
<li><strong>Water throughout.</strong> Even mild dehydration dents strength and focus.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p28"><h2>Supplements: what works, what is marketing</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p29">None of these are required. Food, training and sleep do the work; supplements only fill gaps. But a few earn their place:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p30"><ul>
<li><strong>Creatine monohydrate — 3 to 5 g a day.</strong> The one supplement with strong, repeated evidence for strength and muscle, and safe long-term at that dose (Kreider et al., 2017; Antonio et al., 2021). Cheap and boring. Take it daily, timing irrelevant.</li>
<li><strong>Whey protein.</strong> Not magic — just a convenient, complete source of protein when meals fall short. A tool, not a requirement.</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin D.</strong> Worth supplementing if you are low, which is common. It is about general health, not a muscle shortcut.</li>
<li><strong>Zinc and magnesium.</strong> Here is the honest part: unless you are actually deficient, these do nothing for testosterone, muscle or sleep. Recent trials found a popular "ZMA" blend had no benefit for sleep or performance in people who already had enough (Gallagher et al., 2024). Fix your diet before you fix it with a pill.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p31"><h2>Cardio and steps: the quiet fat-loss lever</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p32">You do not need hours on a treadmill. For health and longevity, the step research points to a benefit that rises with movement and then flattens out at roughly <strong>7,000 to 8,000 steps a day</strong> for most adults (Paluch et al., 2022). For fat loss, those steps are cheap calories burned that never tax your recovery the way heavy cardio does. Start there. Add one or two easy sessions a week if you enjoy them — just do not let excessive cardio eat into the recovery your lifting depends on.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p33"><h2>Sleep: the lever that decides fat versus muscle</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p34">If you change nothing else, this is where to look. In a controlled study, people who cut sleep from about 8.5 hours to 5.5 hours lost the same amount of weight — but far more of it came from muscle instead of fat, and their hunger went up (Nedeltcheva et al., 2010). Sleep does not just help recovery; it changes <em>which tissue</em> you lose. Aim for <strong>7 to 9 hours</strong> on a consistent schedule (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015), with a fixed wake time doing more for you than a perfect bedtime.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p35"><h2>Put it together: your first 30 days</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p36">Do not try to change everything at once. Lock in a few defaults and let them run:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p37"><ul>
<li>Lift three to four times a week, and write down your numbers.</li>
<li>Hit your protein target most days — a palm or two at every meal.</li>
<li>Walk 8,000 steps, sleep seven hours, on a fixed wake time.</li>
<li>Pick your energy direction: a small deficit to lose fat, a small surplus to build, or maintenance to recompose.</li>
<li>Track the scale's weekly average, your lifts and a monthly photo — never the daily number alone.</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_body-recomposition-guide_p38">The body follows the routine, and the routine follows the identity. By day thirty you are not "someone trying to get in shape" — you are someone who trains, eats enough protein, and shows up on the bad days too. That is the actual result. The mirror catches up later.</div>
