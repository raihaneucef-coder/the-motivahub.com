faq:
  - q: "How does dehydration affect physical performance?"
    a: "Less tidy than the poster version. A meta-analysis of dehydration studies found that losing 2-4% of body mass as fluid did not meaningfully change time-trial performance (about +0.1% on average), while fixed-intensity tests shortened by roughly 2% (Goulet, <em>Br J Sports Med</em> 2013, <a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22763119/\">PubMed 22763119</a>). What does drop consistently is how hard the work feels, and cognition: visual vigilance, working-memory speed and mood degraded at about 1.5% body-mass loss in young men (Ganio, <em>Br J Nutr</em> 2011, <a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21736786/\">PubMed 21736786</a>). Dehydration costs focus and comfort before it costs your race."
  - q: "How much water should I drink during exercise?"
    a: "Aim to keep body-mass loss under about 2% during a session. Small, frequent amounts work best — roughly 150-250 ml every 15-20 minutes is what most stomachs tolerate during hard effort (ACSM position stand, <a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/\">PubMed 17277604</a>). Weigh yourself before and after and drink back the difference; when you train again within 24 hours, sports-medicine practice is to replace roughly 125-150% of the loss, since you keep losing water in urine. Thirst is a usable cue on ordinary days, but it arrives late in long, hot efforts — and later still as you age."
  - q: "Do electrolytes matter for hydration?"
    a: "Yes, once the session runs past 60-90 minutes or you sweat heavily. Plain water in large volume over several hours can dilute blood sodium — that is how hyponatremia happens, rare but serious. A pinch of salt plus a squeeze of lemon, or an electrolyte tab (300-500 mg sodium), covers the common case. What it will not reliably do is stop cramps: the evidence on cramps is mixed."

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p2">Losing around 2% of your body mass in fluid reliably costs concentration and makes the same work feel harder. What it costs in endurance depends on how endurance is measured: fixed-intensity tests shorten, while time-trial setups show only a small average decrement (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22763119/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Goulet, Br J Sports Med 2013</a>). Water is still the simplest, cheapest and most effective performance enhancer available — it just deserves a more precise sales pitch.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p3">The usual rule of thumb is three days without water against three weeks without food — a rough survival comparison, not a measured one. The everyday gap is quieter than that: many people drift through afternoons under-hydrated and blame the tiredness on everything else.</div>


<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p4"><h2>How Dehydration Affects You</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p5">- <strong>~2% body-mass loss</strong> — endurance output and concentration start to measurably drop
- <strong>3–5% loss</strong> — the decrements grow, and heat makes them bite sooner
- <strong>Strength and power</strong> — less consistently affected; the tidy "every 2% costs 10% of strength" ladder that circulates online is not what the reviews show</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p6">Mild dehydration is measurable before it is dramatic: at about 1.5% body-mass loss in a warm room, visual vigilance, working-memory speed and mood all degraded in healthy young men (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21736786/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ganio et al., Br J Nutr 2011</a>). That same dip is often what <a href="/journal/gestion-energie/">afternoon energy</a> turns out to be.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p7"><h2>How Much Water Do You Need?</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p8">There is no official per-kilo rule. The EFSA panel sets adequate intake at about 2.0 litres per day for adult women and 2.5 litres for adult men, water from food included (<a href="https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459" target="_blank" rel="noopener">EFSA, 2010</a>). The coaching shorthand of 35 ml per kg — 2.45 litres at 70 kg — lands in the same band, but it is a shortcut, not a recommendation. Heat, session length, sweat rate and body size move the real number.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p9">A simple test: if your urine is pale yellow, you are hydrated. If it is dark yellow, drink more.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p20">That check needs no equipment: urine pale like lemonade rather than dark like apple juice is the rough, widely used public-health shorthand for "probably hydrated". Concentrated and dark means drink now — and take the rest of the afternoon seriously. For the science of the attention dip behind mid-afternoon fog, see <a href="/journal/water-before-willpower/">Water Before Willpower</a>.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p10"><h2>When to Drink</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p11"><h3>1. First Thing in the Morning</h3>
You lose water overnight, and 500 ml first thing replaces it. The metabolic "kick" is real but small: the original German study reported metabolic rate rising about 30% for under an hour after 500 ml — roughly 100 kJ, much of it explained by warming the water to body temperature (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14671205/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Boschmann et al., J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2003</a>). Independent reassessments found the effect marginal, under 3% of resting energy expenditure (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26690288/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Charrière et al., Nutrition & Diabetes 2015</a>). Drink it for hydration and for the routine, not as a fat-loss trick.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p12"><h3>2. Before Meals</h3>
Drinking water before meals helps with digestion and can reduce appetite.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p13"><h3>3. During Exercise</h3>
Small and frequent wins: roughly 150-250 ml every 15-20 minutes is what most stomachs tolerate during hard effort. The working target is to finish no more than about 2% down on body mass (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ACSM position stand, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2007</a>). Under an hour in mild weather, thirst alone is usually enough.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p14"><h3>4. After Exercise</h3>
Replace the fluid you lost. Weigh yourself before and after: about 1.5 ml of fluid per gram lost is the usual target when you train again within 24 hours, because you keep losing water in urine; otherwise ordinary meals and drinks close the gap (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ACSM position stand, 2007</a>).</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p15"><h2>Tips to Drink More</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_hydratation-performance_p16">1. Carry a water bottle everywhere
2. Set hourly reminders
3. Drink a glass of water before every meal
4. Flavor your water with lemon or cucumber
5. Eat water-rich foods (watermelon, cucumber, oranges)</div>
