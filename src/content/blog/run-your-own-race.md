<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p11"><h2>Why Going Out Too Fast Costs You the Race</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p12">Every runner learns the same brutal lesson: the speed you borrow in the first kilometer, you repay with interest in the last. The ones who surge at the start feel fast for 5K. By 30K they are walking. This is not a motivation problem — it is physiology. A pace you cannot hold for the whole distance was never your pace; it was a fantasy rented from your future self. The same thing happens to anyone who front-loads a project, a career, or a budget: the early applause gets paid back as a crash.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p13">So the first job is not to get faster. It is to find the pace you can actually sustain — then defend it even when it looks slow.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p6">This is not just sport. It is career, it is saving, it is building anything that matters. The person who sprints at the start is performing. The person who holds their pace is competing.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p14"><h2>What Running Your Own Race Looks Like Off the Track</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p15">Career: the person who "got lucky" at 30 has usually been putting in steady, unglamorous reps for a decade. Building: the founder who compounds growth quietly tends to beat the one who spikes and then stalls. Money: the investor who holds a boring plan for twenty years outlasts the one chasing this month's winner. In every case the losers are not less talented — they simply lost the will to hold a pace that looked slow while everyone else sprinted.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p7">Pacing feels slow. It feels like you are being left behind. But the race is not won at the start. It is won by the person who is <a href="/journal/recovery-is-training/">still running when others have burned out</a>.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p16"><h2>The Internal Scorecard</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p17">How do you know you are holding your pace when you are not allowed to look at where everyone else is? You measure against your own splits, not their position. Write your baseline down. Track this week against last week. The only comparison that makes a race faster is your current split versus your previous one — everything else is noise. And if the real problem is the comparing habit itself rather than pacing, that is a different fight: read <a href="/journal/comparison-trap/">the comparison trap</a> for it.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p8">Practice it this week:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p9">- <strong>Know your pace.</strong> What is sustainable for you, not for the person next to you?
- <strong>Ignore the first surge.</strong> Let them go. Your race is longer.
- <strong>Finish strong.</strong> The last kilometer is where pacing pays.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p18"><h2>How to Find the Pace You Can Actually Hold</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p19">Pace is not guessed, it is measured. Do a few easy sessions and notice the effort you can sustain while still breathing through your nose — that is your real baseline, not the number you saw someone else post. Set targets from that baseline, then add five to ten percent once a week starts feeling easy. The same logic builds careers and projects: commit to the volume you can repeat on your worst day, and grow from consistency, not from heroics. For the daily system that keeps you honest, see <a href="/journal/discipline-personnelle-guide/">the personal discipline guide</a>.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_run-your-own-race_p10">You do not need to be the fastest at the start. You need to be the one who is still running at the end.</div>
