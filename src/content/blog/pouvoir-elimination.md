<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p2">I deleted 80% of my to-do list in January 2025. Not because I ran out of time — because I finally counted where it was actually going.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p3">We live in a culture that celebrates busyness. More tasks, more meetings, more emails, more everything. But the most productive people do not do more — they do less.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p4">They eliminate ruthlessly. They focus only on what matters. And the results speak for themselves.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p5"><h2>The Pareto Principle</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p6">The 80/20 rule states that 80% of your results come from 20% of your efforts. This means most of what you do does not matter. I measured mine one ordinary week: of fourteen recurring meetings, exactly two produced a decision I can trace. Of the "productivity" tools I opened daily, one earned its place. The math is brutal — and it generalizes.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p7"><ul>
<li>80% of your meetings are unnecessary</li>
<li>80% of your emails do not require a response</li>
<li>80% of your tasks do not move you toward your goals</li>
</ul></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p8">The question is: which 20% actually matters?</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p18"><h2>The 3-Question Elimination Filter</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p19">Before anything gets cut, it has to survive three questions. I took them from a mix of Newport, Drucker, and hard-won experience:</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p20"><ol>
<li><strong>If I weren't already doing this, would I start it today?</strong> Sunk cost disguises itself as commitment all the time.</li>
<li><strong>Does this directly serve my #1 goal this quarter?</strong> Not "is it mildly useful" — does it feed the priority.</li>
<li><strong>What actually happens if I stop entirely?</strong> Usually nothing. The fear does all the sitting.</li>
</ol></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p21">I ran this filter on a subscription I'd kept "forever," a Tuesday standing meeting, and a side project. The subscription: nobody missed it — including me. The meeting: cancelled after my first no-show. The side project took me three months to admit was dead, and question one would have killed it in three minutes.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p9"><h2>How to Eliminate</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p10"><h3>1. Audit Your Time</h3>
For one week, track how you spend every hour. You will be shocked at how much time goes to low-value activities. My first audit surfaced eleven weekly hours between "checking what everyone is up to" and reorganizing my task list — activity that felt like work and produced nothing.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p11"><h3>2. Apply the 80/20 Rule</h3>
Identify the 20% of activities that produce 80% of your results. Focus there. Eliminate or delegate the rest. Mine turned out to be writing and one weekly planning session; everything else was shrinkable. Yours might be two client calls and the craft at the center of your job.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p12"><h3>3. Learn to Say No</h3>
Every yes is a no to something else. Say no to meetings that could be emails. Say no to projects that do not align with your goals. Say no to requests that drain your energy. I use a two-word script: "not now." If something is genuinely important, "now" will come find it on its own.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p13"><h3>4. Automate or Delegate</h3>
If a task does not require your unique skills, automate it or delegate it. Your time is best spent on high-value work. Repetition becomes a template, a template becomes an automation: my weekly report went from a writing session to a four-minute script pass, twice a month.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p14"><h3>5. Embrace Boredom</h3>
When you eliminate distractions, you might feel bored. That is good. Boredom is where creativity lives. Every decent idea I have had in two years arrived during a deliberately empty twenty minutes — no phone, no podcast, just a walk.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p22"><h2>Build Your Stop-Doing List</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p23">A to-do list adds. A stop-doing list subtracts — and subtraction compounds. Mine lives at the top of my notes app and gets reviewed every Sunday. Once an item is on it, it can only come back with a written reason.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p26"><h2>What Elimination Feels Like (Week One)</h2></div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p27">Let me be honest about the first days: uncomfortable. Days 2-4 you feel guilty, like you are skipping work. Day 5, the empty calendar itches and you nearly add junk back to fill it. That restlessness is busyness withdrawal — it passes.</div>

<div data-i18n-html="article_pouvoir-elimination_p28">By week two something flips. The freed hours start producing more than the crowded ones ever did, and the paradox makes sense: doing less is not laziness rewarded. It is concentration, finally unburied.</div>
